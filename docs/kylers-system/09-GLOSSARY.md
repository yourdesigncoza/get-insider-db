# Glossary

Every investing term used in this guide, explained so a smart 12-year-old could understand it. Each term gets a simple definition, then a "Think of it like..." analogy to make it stick.

---

### Balance Sheet

A financial statement that shows everything a company owns (assets), everything it owes (liabilities), and the difference between them (equity). It's a snapshot of the company's financial position on one specific day.

*Think of it like... a report card for your piggy bank. On one side: how much money and stuff you have. On the other side: how much you owe people. The difference is what's truly "yours."*

---

### Bear Market

When the stock market or a particular stock has fallen 20% or more from its recent high. Investors are pessimistic and scared, so prices keep dropping.

*Think of it like... a school where everyone suddenly thinks the cafeteria food is terrible. Even the kids who liked it stop eating there because everyone else is leaving. The negativity feeds on itself.*

---

### Bull Market

When the stock market or a particular stock has been rising steadily. Investors are optimistic and confident, so prices keep climbing.

*Think of it like... the opposite of a bear market. Everyone thinks the cafeteria food is amazing, and more people keep showing up to eat there, making it even more popular.*

---

### CAGR (Compound Annual Growth Rate)

The average yearly growth rate of an investment over a specific period, assuming profits are reinvested. If $100 grows to $200 over 3 years, the CAGR is about 26% -- not 33% (which would be the simple average), because each year's growth compounds on the previous year's.

*Think of it like... a snowball rolling downhill. Each year it doesn't just add the same amount of snow -- it gets bigger faster because there's more surface area to collect snow. CAGR measures that accelerating growth as a smooth annual percentage.*

---

### Capex (Capital Expenditures)

Money a company spends on long-term assets like buildings, machinery, equipment, or technology. This is different from everyday operating expenses like salaries or rent.

*Think of it like... the difference between buying groceries (operating expense) and buying a new oven (capex). Groceries get used up immediately; the oven lasts for years and helps you make more food.*

---

### Cash Flow

The movement of money in and out of a business. Positive cash flow means more money coming in than going out. See also: Free Cash Flow, Operating Cash Flow.

*Think of it like... water flowing through a bathtub. The faucet is money coming in, the drain is money going out. You want the faucet running faster than the drain.*

---

### Circle of Competence

The range of businesses and industries that you genuinely understand well enough to make informed investment decisions. Staying inside your circle means only investing in what you truly comprehend.

*Think of it like... subjects in school. You might be great at math and science but terrible at art. If someone asked you to grade art projects, you'd make mistakes. Stick to grading what you actually know.*

---

### Competitive Advantage

Something a company does better than its rivals that lets it earn higher profits or attract more customers. Unlike an economic moat, a competitive advantage may not be permanent.

*Think of it like... being the fastest kid in your class. Right now, you win every race. But next year, someone taller might show up and beat you. It's an advantage today, but it might not last.*

---

### Concentration

Putting a large portion of your money into a small number of investments rather than spreading it across many. Kyler's portfolio holds a maximum of 12 positions -- that's concentrated compared to an index fund holding 500 stocks.

*Think of it like... betting big on a few answers you're confident about on a test rather than guessing on all of them. Higher potential score, but each wrong answer hurts more.*

---

### Conviction

How confident you are in an investment thesis, backed by deep research and understanding. High conviction means you've done extensive work and believe strongly that your analysis is correct.

*Think of it like... the difference between guessing the answer on a test and knowing it because you studied for hours. Both might be right, but conviction comes from preparation.*

---

### Cost Basis

The original price you paid for an investment, including any fees. Used to calculate your profit or loss when you sell.

*Think of it like... the receipt for something you bought. If you bought a video game for $40 and sold it for $60, your cost basis is $40 and your profit is $20.*

---

### Current Ratio

A measure of whether a company can pay its bills coming due in the next 12 months. Calculated by dividing current assets (cash, inventory, receivables) by current liabilities (bills due within a year). A ratio above 1.0 means the company has more short-term assets than short-term debts.

*Think of it like... checking if you have enough allowance saved to cover the things you've promised to buy this month. Above 1.0 = you're covered. Below 1.0 = you might need to borrow.*

---

### Cyclical

A business or industry whose performance rises and falls with the broader economy or a specific economic cycle. Auto sales, construction, and energy are cyclical -- they boom during good times and bust during bad times.

*Think of it like... an ice cream stand. Summer is great (boom), winter is terrible (bust), but summer always comes back. The business isn't broken during winter -- it's just in its down cycle.*

---

### DCF (Discounted Cash Flow)

A valuation method that estimates what a company is worth today by projecting all the cash it will generate in the future and then "discounting" it back to today's value (because a dollar today is worth more than a dollar in five years). Kyler prefers simpler methods but DCF is a common tool.

*Think of it like... someone promising you $100 a year from now. That promise is worth less than $100 in your hand today, because you could invest today's $100 and have more than $100 by next year. DCF does this math for a whole company's future cash.*

---

### Deep Value

An investing style focused on buying stocks at prices dramatically below their intrinsic worth -- not just "a little cheap" but profoundly underpriced, usually because the market has overreacted to bad news.

*Think of it like... finding a $100 bill on the ground that everyone walked past because it was crumpled and dirty. It's still worth $100 -- they just couldn't see it.*

---

### Dilution

When a company issues new shares of stock, reducing the ownership percentage of existing shareholders. Each existing share represents a smaller piece of the company.

*Think of it like... cutting a pizza into more slices without making the pizza bigger. Everyone's slice gets smaller, even though the pizza itself hasn't changed.*

---

### Diversification

Spreading investments across different companies, industries, or asset types to reduce risk. If one investment does poorly, others may offset the loss.

*Think of it like... not putting all your eggs in one basket. If you drop one basket, you lose everything. With multiple baskets, one dropped basket only costs you some eggs.*

---

### Dividend

A payment a company makes to its shareholders, usually from profits. Typically paid quarterly (every 3 months). Not all companies pay dividends -- many reinvest their profits back into the business instead.

*Think of it like... a landlord collecting rent and sending you a check each quarter because you own part of the building. The building is the company; the rent check is the dividend.*

---

### Dividend Yield

The annual dividend payment divided by the stock price, expressed as a percentage. A $100 stock paying $3/year in dividends has a 3% dividend yield.

*Think of it like... the "interest rate" on your investment from dividends alone. A 3% yield means for every $100 you invest, you get $3 per year in cash payments.*

---

### EBITDA

Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortization. A measure of a company's operating performance that strips out financing decisions, tax differences, and accounting adjustments. Useful for comparing companies with different debt levels.

*Think of it like... measuring how much your lemonade stand earns before you account for the loan you took to buy the stand, the taxes you owe, and the wear-and-tear on your equipment. It shows the raw earning power of the business itself.*

---

### Economic Moat

A *sustainable* competitive advantage that protects a company from competitors over the long term -- like the moat around a castle that keeps invaders out. Different from a regular competitive advantage because it lasts.

**Types of moats (with examples from Kyler's writing):**

| Moat Type | How It Works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Low Cost** | Company can sell cheaper than anyone else and still profit | Costco (12.6% gross margin vs 25.8% industry average) |
| **Regulation** | Government rules make it nearly impossible for new competitors to enter | Union Pacific (building a new railroad requires dozens of approvals) |
| **Switching Cost** | It's too expensive or painful for customers to leave | Microsoft (businesses deeply integrated with Office/Teams) |
| **Network Effect** | The product gets more valuable as more people use it | Meta (more users = more value for each user) |
| **Capital Requirements** | So expensive to enter the industry that few can afford to try | Boeing (a single plane can cost $450 million to build) |

> "An economic moat is long-lasting protection a business has from outside competitors taking market share or profits away from them. When you find a business that has one, it feels hard to lose."

---

### Enterprise Value (EV)

The total value of a company including both its stock market value (market cap) and its debt, minus cash. This gives a more complete picture than market cap alone because it accounts for how the company is financed.

*Think of it like... the true price of a house. The "market cap" is what the homeowner's equity is worth. But the house also has a mortgage (debt). Enterprise value = equity + mortgage - cash in the piggy bank.*

---

### EPS (Earnings Per Share)

A company's total profit divided by the number of shares outstanding. If a company earns $10 million and has 1 million shares, EPS is $10.

*Think of it like... splitting the total class prize money equally among all students. EPS tells you how much profit each share "earns."*

---

### EV/EBITDA

Enterprise value divided by EBITDA. A valuation ratio that tells you how many years of operating earnings it would take to equal the company's total value. Lower numbers generally mean cheaper, but it varies by industry.

*Think of it like... if you're buying a business, how many years of the business's earnings would it take to pay off what you paid? An EV/EBITDA of 8 means "8 years of earnings to pay back the purchase price."*

---

### EV/FCF

Enterprise value divided by free cash flow. Similar to EV/EBITDA but uses actual cash generated rather than an accounting measure. Kyler uses this as one of his core valuation metrics.

*Think of it like... same as EV/EBITDA, but instead of looking at operating earnings, you're looking at the actual cash the business spits out. Often a more reliable measure.*

---

### FCF (Free Cash Flow)

The cash a company generates from operations after spending what it needs to maintain and grow the business (capital expenditures). FCF = Operating Cash Flow minus Capex. This is "real money" the company can use for dividends, buybacks, debt repayment, or reinvestment.

*Think of it like... your allowance minus the money you have to spend on school supplies. What's left over is your free cash -- money you can actually choose what to do with.*

---

### FCF Margin

Free cash flow divided by revenue, expressed as a percentage. Shows what percentage of every dollar of revenue becomes free cash. A 10% FCF margin means for every $100 in revenue, $10 becomes free cash.

*Think of it like... if your lemonade stand makes $100 in sales and you have $10 left after all expenses and equipment, your FCF margin is 10%.*

---

### FCF Multiple

Another term for P/FCF (Price-to-Free-Cash-Flow). The stock price divided by FCF per share, or equivalently, market cap divided by total FCF. Tells you how many years of current FCF the market is "paying" for the stock.

*Think of it like... if a business generates $10/year in free cash and you pay $100 for it, you're paying a 10x FCF multiple -- it would take 10 years of cash flow to "earn back" your purchase price (assuming no growth).*

---

### FCF Yield

Free cash flow per share divided by the stock price, expressed as a percentage. The inverse of the FCF multiple. A 5% FCF yield means the company generates $5 in free cash for every $100 of stock price.

*Think of it like... the "cash interest rate" your investment earns. A 5% FCF yield is like a savings account paying 5% -- except instead of interest, it's cash the business generates.*

---

### Float (Insurance)

Money that insurance companies collect from policyholders as premiums and hold until claims need to be paid. During the holding period, the company invests this money to earn returns. It's essentially free borrowed money when managed well.

*Think of it like... your parents giving you their grocery money on Monday but not needing it until Friday. For those five days, you could put it in a savings account and earn interest -- then give them back the original amount. Insurance companies do this on a massive scale.*

---

### Fundamentals

The core financial and operational data about a company: revenue, profits, cash flow, debt, growth rate, margins, competitive position, and management quality. As opposed to "technical analysis" which looks at stock price patterns.

*Think of it like... reading the actual report card instead of just guessing how smart someone is based on their popularity. Fundamentals are the real numbers underneath the stock price.*

---

### Gross Margin

Revenue minus the direct cost of producing goods or services (called "cost of goods sold"), divided by revenue. Shows how much money is left after making the product but before overhead, salaries, rent, etc.

*Think of it like... if you sell lemonade for $1 per cup and the lemons, sugar, and cups cost $0.40, your gross margin is 60%. You still have other costs to pay, but this tells you how much money the product itself generates.*

---

### Hurdle Rate

The minimum annual return an investor requires before committing money. Kyler's hurdle rate is 30% per year -- if a stock can't reasonably deliver that, he passes.

*Think of it like... a high-jump bar. If an investment can't clear the bar, it doesn't make the team. Setting the bar high means you only invest in the best opportunities.*

---

### Implied Volatility

A measure of how much the market expects a stock's price to move in the future, derived from options prices. Higher implied volatility = the market expects bigger price swings = options are more expensive.

*Think of it like... weather forecasts for stock prices. If the "forecast" says big storms (high implied volatility), umbrella insurance (options) costs more. Kyler prefers to buy options when the forecast is calm (average or low implied volatility).*

---

### Index Fund

A fund that buys all (or a representative sample of) the stocks in a market index, like the S&P 500. Instead of picking individual stocks, you own a tiny piece of every company in the index. Very low fees, widely recommended for most people.

*Think of it like... instead of buying one flavor of ice cream and hoping you picked the best one, you buy a sampler of all 500 flavors. You'll never have the single best flavor, but you'll never have the worst either.*

---

### Intrinsic Value

What a company is actually worth based on its fundamentals, as opposed to what the stock market currently prices it at. The goal of value investing is to buy stocks trading below their intrinsic value.

*Think of it like... the real worth of your baseball card collection based on what collectors would pay, not whatever random price your friend offers you at lunch. The market price might be higher or lower than the true worth.*

---

### Leverage

Using borrowed money (debt) to increase the potential return on investment. Also used more broadly to describe a company's total debt relative to its equity or earnings.

*Think of it like... using a bigger lever to lift a heavier rock. Leverage amplifies your results -- both gains AND losses. A little leverage can boost returns; too much leverage can crush you.*

---

### Liquidity

How quickly and easily an asset can be converted to cash without significantly affecting its price. Cash is the most liquid asset. A house is illiquid (takes months to sell). For stocks, liquidity means how easily you can buy or sell shares.

*Think of it like... the difference between having cash in your pocket (very liquid) and owning a rare comic book (illiquid). You can spend cash instantly, but selling the comic takes time and you might not get the price you want.*

---

### Long (Position)

Owning a stock with the expectation that its price will go up. When people say "I'm long XYZ," they mean they own shares and are betting on the stock rising.

*Think of it like... buying something at a store expecting to sell it later for more. You "go long" because you believe the value will increase over time.*

---

### Margin of Safety

The gap between what you pay for a stock and what you believe it's truly worth. The bigger the gap, the more room for error in your analysis. If you think a stock is worth $100 and you buy it at $60, you have a 40% margin of safety.

*Think of it like... a bridge rated for 20 tons that you only drive 10-ton trucks across. Even if your weight estimate is a little off, you're still safe. In investing, you buy cheap enough that even if you're somewhat wrong about the value, you probably won't lose money.*

---

### Market Cap (Market Capitalization)

The total stock market value of a company. Calculated by multiplying the current share price by the total number of shares outstanding. A $50 stock with 10 million shares = $500 million market cap.

*Think of it like... the price tag on the entire company if you could buy every single share. It tells you how big the market thinks the company is.*

---

### Mr. Market

A metaphor invented by Benjamin Graham (Warren Buffett's teacher). Imagine the stock market as a person named "Mr. Market" who shows up at your door every day offering to buy your stocks or sell you his -- but his mood swings wildly. Some days he's euphoric and offers crazy high prices. Other days he's depressed and offers fire-sale prices. You don't have to trade with him. The key insight is that *his price offers tell you about his mood, not about what the stocks are actually worth.*

*Think of it like... a kid in your class who offers to trade Pokemon cards every day but never offers fair deals. Some days he overvalues everything, some days he panics and undervalues everything. You just wait for the days when he's selling great cards cheap.*

---

### Multiple

A shorthand for valuation ratios like P/E, P/FCF, or EV/EBITDA. When someone says "the stock trades at a 15x multiple," they mean the price is 15 times the underlying metric (earnings, cash flow, etc.).

*Think of it like... a multiplier on a game score. A 15x multiple means the market is willing to pay 15 times the company's annual earnings to own the stock.*

---

### Narrative

The story the market tells itself about a company. Narratives drive stock prices in the short term -- "this company is failing," "this sector is dead," "AI will change everything." Narratives can disconnect dramatically from the actual fundamentals.

*Think of it like... rumors at school. Sometimes the rumor is true, sometimes it's completely wrong, but it still affects how everyone treats the person. Kyler looks for companies where the narrative is wrong -- where the "rumor" doesn't match reality.*

---

### Normalized (Margins/Earnings)

Adjusting current financial metrics to reflect what they would look like under normal business conditions. If a company's FCF margin is usually 8% but fell to 2% during a downturn, the "normalized" margin is closer to 8%.

*Think of it like... judging a runner's speed based on their typical race times, not the one race where they had the flu. Normalization strips out temporary distortions to show what "normal" looks like.*

---

### Operating Cash Flow

Cash generated from a company's core business operations -- selling products, providing services. Excludes cash from investments, loans, or selling assets. The starting point for calculating FCF.

*Think of it like... the money your lemonade stand actually brings in from selling lemonade. Doesn't count money you borrowed from your parents or earned by selling your old bike.*

---

### Options (Call/Long-dated)

Financial contracts that give you the *right* (but not the obligation) to buy a stock at a specific price before a specific date. A **call option** lets you buy; you profit when the stock goes up. **Long-dated** means the expiration date is far in the future (12+ months), giving you more time to be right.

*Think of it like... paying $5 for the right to buy a concert ticket at $50, even if the ticket price rises to $100 later. Your $5 gets you access to a potentially huge gain. But if the ticket price stays below $50, your $5 is lost.*

---

### P/E Ratio (Price-to-Earnings)

Stock price divided by earnings per share. The most common valuation metric. A P/E of 20 means investors are paying $20 for every $1 of annual earnings.

*Think of it like... if a business earns $1,000 per year and someone offers to buy it for $20,000, that's a 20x P/E. It would take 20 years of current earnings to "earn back" the purchase price.*

---

### P/FCF (Price-to-Free-Cash-Flow)

Stock price divided by free cash flow per share. Kyler's preferred valuation metric because FCF represents real cash, unlike earnings which can be distorted by accounting.

*Think of it like... P/E but using actual cash generated instead of accounting profits. Cash is harder to fake.*

---

### P/S (Price-to-Sales)

Stock price divided by revenue per share. Useful for valuing companies that aren't yet profitable, since they still have revenue even without earnings.

*Think of it like... if your lemonade stand does $1,000 in sales and I offer to buy it for $5,000, that's a 5x P/S ratio. Useful when the stand isn't profitable yet but clearly has demand.*

---

### Position Sizing

How much money to put into a single investment as a percentage of your total portfolio. Kyler sizes positions based on his conviction level and downside risk assessment.

*Think of it like... deciding how many chips to bet on a poker hand. Better hand (higher conviction, lower risk) = bigger bet. Uncertain hand = smaller bet. Never bet everything on one hand.*

---

### Price Target

The price at which an investor believes a stock is fairly valued or would be worth buying. Kyler sets price targets on his watchlist so he gets alerted when a stock drops to a level he finds attractive.

*Think of it like... the maximum price you'd pay for a used car. You might love the car at $15,000 but refuse to pay $25,000. Your price target is $15,000.*

---

### Quick Ratio

Similar to current ratio but excludes inventory (which might not sell quickly). Calculated as (current assets minus inventory) divided by current liabilities. A stricter test of whether a company can pay its short-term bills.

*Think of it like... the same as the current ratio test, but you can't count unsold merchandise. Only cash and money people owe you. A tougher standard.*

---

### Rerating

When the market changes the valuation multiple it assigns to a stock. A "rerating higher" means investors are suddenly willing to pay more per dollar of earnings. This often happens when sentiment shifts -- from "this company is dying" to "this company is recovering."

*Think of it like... a student who always got C's suddenly starts getting A's. Teachers (the market) change their opinion and start expecting great things. The student's "rating" goes up, even before seeing the next report card.*

---

### ROCE (Return on Capital Employed)

A measure of how much profit a company generates from all the capital tied up in the business (both equity and debt). ROCE = Operating Profit / Capital Employed.

*Think of it like... if someone gave you $1,000 (some your own money, some borrowed) to run a business, and you earned $150 in profit, your ROCE is 15%. Higher is better -- it means you're efficient with the total capital at your disposal.*

---

### ROE (Return on Equity)

How much profit a company makes from just the shareholders' money (not including debt). ROE = Net Income / Shareholders' Equity. Most useful when debt isn't a major factor.

*Think of it like... if you invest $100 of YOUR money (no borrowing) and earn $20, your ROE is 20%. It measures how well the company uses the owners' money specifically.*

---

### ROI (Return on Investment)

The most basic return measure: how much profit you made relative to what you invested. ROI = (Gain - Cost) / Cost. If you buy a stock for $50 and sell for $75, your ROI is 50%.

*Think of it like... the scoreboard for any investment. "I put in X, I got back Y, and the difference is my return."*

---

### ROIC (Return on Invested Capital)

How much operating profit (after taxes) a company generates from all invested capital (equity + debt - cash). The most comprehensive return metric because it includes both debt and equity and excludes idle cash.

*Think of it like... ROCE's more precise cousin. It takes all money actually put to work in the business and measures what it earns. Kyler considers this one of the most important metrics. Generally wants to see above 6% minimum, ideally 10%+.*

---

### S&P 500

An index of the 500 largest publicly traded companies in the United States, weighted by market cap. Widely used as the benchmark for "the stock market." When people say "the market returned 10%," they often mean the S&P 500.

*Think of it like... the "top 500" leaderboard for American companies. It represents the overall health and direction of the US stock market.*

---

### Secular Decline

A long-term, structural shrinking of an industry, not caused by temporary economic conditions. DVD rental, print newspapers, and horse-drawn carriages are in secular decline.

*Think of it like... an ice cream flavor that fewer people like every year. It's not a temporary dip -- the trend is permanently downward. Kyler avoids industries in secular decline because "rowing against a river can be done but is far more difficult than moving with the current."*

---

### Sentiment

The overall attitude or mood of investors toward a stock, sector, or the market as a whole. Positive sentiment = optimism, higher prices. Negative sentiment = pessimism, lower prices. Sentiment often overshoots in both directions.

*Think of it like... the "vibe" at a party. If everyone's having fun, more people want to come (positive sentiment). If rumors spread that the party is boring, people start leaving (negative sentiment). The actual quality of the party might not have changed at all.*

---

### Share Buyback

When a company uses its cash to repurchase its own shares from the market, reducing the total number of shares outstanding. Each remaining share now represents a larger piece of the company. The opposite of dilution.

*Think of it like... if your pizza has 8 slices and the company buys back 2 slices and destroys them, the remaining 6 slices each represent a bigger portion of the pizza. Your slice got bigger without you doing anything.*

---

### Short-term vs Long-term

In investing, "short-term" generally means days to months; "long-term" means years to decades. Stock prices are driven by sentiment and noise in the short term, but by fundamentals in the long term. Kyler invests with a 1-4 year time horizon.

*Think of it like... weather vs climate. Tomorrow's weather (short-term) is hard to predict. But you can be pretty confident that July will be hotter than December (long-term). Kyler invests based on the "climate," not tomorrow's weather.*

---

### Solvency

A company's ability to meet its long-term financial obligations. A solvent company can pay all its debts as they come due. An insolvent company cannot and may face bankruptcy.

*Think of it like... can you pay your bills not just this month, but for the next several years? If you owe more than you'll ever earn, you're insolvent. This is the first thing Kyler checks.*

---

### Stock-Based Compensation (SBC)

Paying employees (especially executives) with shares of stock instead of (or in addition to) cash. This dilutes existing shareholders because new shares are created. Kyler flags SBC at 5%+ per year as a red flag if the company isn't growing to offset it.

*Think of it like... a company paying its workers by printing new pizza slices instead of paying cash. Everyone else's slices get a little smaller each time. If the pizza is growing fast enough, it's fine. If not, it's a problem.*

---

### Strike Price

The predetermined price at which an option holder can buy (call) or sell (put) the underlying stock. If you hold a call option with a $50 strike, you can buy the stock at $50 regardless of its current market price.

*Think of it like... a coupon that lets you buy a specific item at a locked-in price, no matter what the store charges later.*

---

### Tailwind / Headwind

A **tailwind** is a positive force that helps a company or industry grow (like falling interest rates for homebuilders, or aging cars boosting demand for auto parts). A **headwind** is a negative force that works against it (like rising input costs or regulatory changes).

*Think of it like... riding a bike. A tailwind pushes you forward -- you go faster with less effort. A headwind pushes against you -- you pedal harder just to maintain speed.*

---

### Thesis

An investor's written argument for why a particular investment will succeed. It includes the expected outcome, the catalysts that will drive it, the risks that could derail it, and the time frame. Kyler writes a thesis for every position he takes.

*Think of it like... a book report, but for an investment. It's your complete argument for why this stock will go up, what could go wrong, and how long it should take.*

---

### Time-weighted Returns

A method of calculating investment performance that removes the effect of adding or withdrawing money. This gives a "pure" measure of how well the portfolio's investments actually performed. Kyler uses this to report his returns.

*Think of it like... grading a chef's cooking skill by tasting the food, regardless of whether they cooked for 2 people or 200. It measures the quality of decisions, not the amount of money involved.*

---

### Turnaround

A company that is recovering from a period of poor performance -- declining revenues, margin compression, solvency concerns, management failures, or a combination. Kyler specializes in turnarounds.

*Think of it like... a sports team that had a terrible season, fired the coach, brought in new players, and is starting to win games again. The turnaround is the transition from losing to winning.*

---

### Valuation

The process of estimating what a stock or company is actually worth. Done by comparing the current price to various financial metrics (earnings, cash flow, revenue) and to the company's own history or peers.

*Think of it like... appraising a house. You look at what similar houses sold for, the condition of the house, the neighborhood, and future development plans to decide if the asking price is fair, cheap, or expensive.*

---

### Volatility

How much and how quickly a stock's price moves up and down. High volatility means big price swings; low volatility means the price is relatively stable. Volatility is not the same as risk -- a stock can be volatile and still be a great investment.

*Think of it like... a roller coaster vs a train ride. The roller coaster (high volatility) has big ups and downs, but both get you to the destination. Kyler often buys stocks that are volatile because the volatility creates buying opportunities.*

---

### Watchlist

A list of stocks an investor is monitoring but hasn't yet bought. Kyler's watchlist includes stocks he likes but that are currently too expensive, with price targets set for when he'd consider buying.

*Think of it like... a wish list for your birthday. You're not buying these things yet, but you're keeping track so when the price is right (or someone gives you money), you know exactly what to get.*

---

### YTD (Year-to-Date)

Performance measured from January 1st of the current year to today. "My portfolio is up 25% YTD" means it's gained 25% since the start of this year.

*Think of it like... your grade so far this semester. It's not the final grade yet, but it shows how things are going.*

---

### YoY (Year-over-Year)

Comparing a metric from one period to the same period in the prior year. "Revenue grew 15% YoY" means this quarter's revenue is 15% higher than the same quarter last year.

*Think of it like... comparing your height on this birthday to your height on last year's birthday. It shows how much you've grown in exactly one year, regardless of the season.*
