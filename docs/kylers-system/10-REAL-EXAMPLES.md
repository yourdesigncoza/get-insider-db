# See It in Action: Real Stock Analyses from Kyler's Portfolio

*The framework only matters if it works on real companies with real money. Here are four positions from Kyler's actual portfolio, walked through step by step.*

---

You've read the theory. You know the steps: find ideas, run filters, compare competitors, do the qualitative deep dive, build a valuation, score the decision, size the position, and manage it after you buy.

But reading about a system and *seeing it applied* are two very different things.

This chapter walks through four real investments from Kyler's portfolio. Each one is a different type of turnaround -- a cyclical recovery, a hated duopoly, a geopolitical discount, and a boring staples play. For each stock, you'll see exactly which steps of the framework Kyler used and how he turned research into a decision.

Think of this chapter as the "show your work" section. The math is messy, the estimates are rough, and Kyler will be the first to tell you they'll be wrong to some degree. But the process behind each decision is what separates disciplined investing from guessing.

---

## Example 1: Cooper Standard ($CPS) -- The Auto Parts Turnaround

**What it is:** Cooper Standard makes automotive components -- sealing systems, fuel and brake delivery, fluid transfer, and anti-vibration systems for major automakers like Ford and GM.

**When Kyler first covered it:** August 2024, at around $14.47/share with a $250 million **market cap** (the total value of all a company's shares -- see [09-GLOSSARY.md](09-GLOSSARY.md)).

**Price target:** $193 by 2028

---

### Step 1: Finding the Idea

> **Framework Step: [01-FINDING-IDEAS](01-FINDING-IDEAS.md)** Kyler didn't find Cooper Standard by scrolling through a screener. The credit goes to Thomas Hayes (Hedge Fund Tips on YouTube), one of the external sources Kyler follows for ideas he wouldn't find on his own.

What caught his attention was the chart. Cooper Standard had fallen from $145 to less than $4 -- a **97% drop**. That kind of devastation usually signals one of two things: a company that's dying, or a company that's being priced like it's dying when it isn't.

The auto parts industry is **cyclical** (it goes through regular ups and downs tied to the broader economy -- see [09-GLOSSARY.md](09-GLOSSARY.md)). Kyler knew that auto sales had collapsed during COVID to about 10 million units (roughly the floor in any recession), and were already recovering. That's the top-down lens: a beaten-down industry with a clear catalyst for recovery.

> **Key Idea:** "The foundation of my thesis is the total number of vehicles sold will recover, and you can see we are already on our way." -- Kyler

---

### Step 2: Running the First Filter

> **Framework Step: [02-FIRST-FILTER](02-FIRST-FILTER.md)** Every stock must pass five checks: solvency, dilution, revenue growth, ROCE/ROIC, and rough valuation.

**Solvency -- the biggest question.** In 2021, Cooper Standard burned through $211 million in **free cash flow** (the actual cash left over after running the business and paying for equipment -- see [09-GLOSSARY.md](09-GLOSSARY.md)), leaving just $248 million in cash against $1 billion in debt with $630 million due within three years. Many thought bankruptcy was coming.

But by 2023, things were changing:
- They generated $36 million in positive free cash flow
- They refinanced debt, pushing payments out about 3 years and reducing the amount due by ~30% to $446 million
- They had $266.5 million in available liquidity

Kyler concluded they had enough runway to survive until the auto recovery arrived. That's the solvency check in action: not "is the balance sheet perfect?" but "will this company live long enough for the turnaround to play out?"

**Dilution:** Not a major concern -- share count was stable.

**Revenue growth:** With auto sales recovering from cyclical lows, revenue growth was practically guaranteed by industry tailwinds.

**ROCE/ROIC:** For cyclical companies, Kyler makes exceptions. Returns on capital look terrible at the bottom of a cycle. What matters is whether the company can generate good returns when conditions normalize. Cooper Standard had demonstrated this in prior cycles.

**Rough valuation:** Even at this early stage, the math looked extraordinary -- potential for hundreds of percent upside if the cycle played out. Easily cleared the 30% **CAGR** (Compound Annual Growth Rate -- the average yearly return -- see [09-GLOSSARY.md](09-GLOSSARY.md)) hurdle.

---

### Step 3: Competitor Comparison

> **Framework Step: [03-DEEP-ANALYSIS](03-DEEP-ANALYSIS.md)** Kyler compared Cooper Standard against other auto parts suppliers on leverage, margins, returns on capital, and growth potential.

Cooper Standard stood out for several reasons:
- Massive cost cutting: Over $700 million in costs cut since 2019, translating to more than $100 million in annual savings
- High operating leverage: When revenues grow even slightly, profits multiply. Kyler pointed out that "a mere 2% increase in FCF margins could add $56 million in FCF" -- huge relative to a $250 million market cap
- Future revenue was largely pre-booked: 99% for 2026, 95% for 2027, 85% for 2028
- Named GM Supplier of the Year, signaling quality execution

---

### Step 4: Qualitative Deep Dive

> **Framework Step: [04-QUALITATIVE-DEEP-DIVE](04-QUALITATIVE-DEEP-DIVE.md)** This is where numbers stop being enough.

**Competitive advantages:** While Cooper Standard doesn't have a traditional **moat** (a durable competitive advantage that protects a company from competitors -- see [09-GLOSSARY.md](09-GLOSSARY.md)), it benefits from deep customer relationships with major automakers, patented materials technology, and high switching costs in its niche.

One standout was a deal with Nike. Cooper Standard licensed a patented material called Fortrex to Nike for shoemaking -- a pure-profit revenue stream requiring zero investment. As the CEO put it: "Nike asked us to create a deal that speeds up their product development. We have a royalty stream based on their volumes." An analyst responded: "It's essentially a 100% margin cash flow business." CEO: "Exactly. No investment required from us."

**Management:** Kyler highlighted management's skill in navigating near-death conditions -- cutting $56 million in costs in 2023 alone, on top of $185 million in "sustainable cost reductions" in 2021-2022.

**Catalysts coming down the pipeline:**
- Average age of cars on the road surpassing 13 years (people need new cars)
- Increasing dealership inventories
- Federal Reserve cutting rates (making car loans cheaper)
- Industry recovery from COVID-era lows

---

### Step 5: Valuation

> **Framework Step: [05-VALUATION](05-VALUATION.md)** Kyler's model is always: Revenue x FCF Margin x FCF Multiple / Shares = Price Target.

Here's how he built the Cooper Standard valuation:

| Component | Estimate | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue by 2028-2029 | ~$3.7-3.8B | Management targets: Sealing $1.8B+, Fluid Handling $2B+ |
| FCF Margin | ~6.25% | Slightly above prior cycle peak of 5.8%, reflecting $700M+ in cost cuts |
| Free Cash Flow | ~$231M | Revenue x margin |
| FCF Multiple | 12x | Based on historical peak multiple |
| Implied Market Cap | ~$2.77B | |
| Implied Share Price | ~$160-193 | |
| Upside from ~$14 entry | ~1,000%+ | |
| CAGR | ~62-72% | Depending on time frame |

> **Key Idea:** Even if Kyler's numbers are off by a wide margin, the upside is so large that the investment still works. That's the power of buying quality businesses at extreme discounts. If the target is $160 and he only gets $80, that's still a 5x return from $14.

---

### Step 6: The Decision and Sizing

> **Framework Step: [06-THE-DECISION](06-THE-DECISION.md) and [07-POSITION-SIZING](07-POSITION-SIZING.md)**

With a massive CAGR estimate, a manageable (if still significant) downside risk, and high conviction from the deep research, Cooper Standard scored well enough to become a large position.

Kyler noted in his January 2026 portfolio update that CPS "has significant upside even compared with the risks at 2x, 3x or more my cost basis" -- meaning he'd keep buying even after the stock had already doubled or tripled from his entry.

---

### How It's Playing Out

By January 2026, the thesis was tracking well. U.S. total vehicle sales came in up about 300,000 units in 2025 over 2024, marking the third year of growth after bottoming in 2022. Temporary issues at an aluminum plant affected Q4 volumes, but management expected production to be made up in early 2026. The massive cost-cutting engine continued to deliver.

> **Key Idea:** Turnarounds don't play out in a straight line. There are bumps, delays, and scary quarters. Kyler held through all of them because he understood the business well enough to separate noise from signal. [See 08-AFTER-THE-BUY](08-AFTER-THE-BUY.md) for how he manages positions after purchase.

## Example 2: Boeing ($BA) -- The Hated Duopoly

**What it is:** One of only two companies in the world that makes large commercial aircraft. Also a major defense and space contractor.

**When Kyler covered it in depth:** October 2025, at around $212/share.

**Price target:** $323 by 2027

---

### Step 1: Finding the Idea

> **Framework Step: [01-FINDING-IDEAS](01-FINDING-IDEAS.md)** Boeing is the opposite of an obscure idea -- everyone knows it. What makes it a Kyler-style investment is that everyone *hates* it.

Two fatal 737 MAX crashes (2018-2019), a door plug blowing out mid-flight (2024), production halts, quality scandals, billions in fines, and a stock that cratered. Boeing was one of the most hated companies in the market.

But Kyler saw something critical: Boeing and Airbus are a **duopoly** (only two companies dominate an entire industry). There is no third option for large commercial aircraft. Even at its worst, Boeing's production slots remain valuable because airline executives know that switching to Airbus means waiting up to a decade for delivery.

> **Key Idea:** "No matter how bad things seem, and they are much better than you think, they are backstopped by governments and militaries around the world." -- Kyler

---

### Step 2: Running the First Filter

> **Framework Step: [02-FIRST-FILTER](02-FIRST-FILTER.md)**

**Solvency:** This was the scariest checkbox. Boeing was burning cash, carrying over $53 billion in debt, and had negative free cash flow. But Kyler looked deeper:
- $23 billion in cash on hand
- An additional ~$10 billion coming from the sale of their Jeppesen/Digital Aviation Solutions business
- $10 billion in undrawn credit facilities
- First debt maturity not until 2030, extending all the way to 2064
- A $636 billion backlog (that's over 10 years of work already ordered)

Conclusion: "Liquidity is not a concern anymore."

**Revenue growth:** With 5,900+ aircraft pending delivery, demand wasn't the issue -- supply was. Revenue growth was essentially guaranteed as production ramped up.

**ROCE/ROIC:** Terrible currently, but historically Boeing generated $11-13 billion in annual free cash flow (2017-2018). This was a "return to normal" story.

**Rough valuation:** Even with conservative assumptions, 20%+ annual returns looked achievable.

---

### Step 3: Deep Analysis -- The Production Math

> **Framework Step: [03-DEEP-ANALYSIS](03-DEEP-ANALYSIS.md)**

Boeing's turnaround is essentially a math problem: more planes delivered = more revenue = higher margins (because fixed costs get spread across more units).

Kyler tracked production milestones closely:
- FAA raised the 737 MAX production cap to 42/month (up from 38)
- 787 line targeting 8/month by end of 2025, then 10/month in 2026
- 777X deliveries expected in 2027
- By August 2025, Boeing had delivered more planes than all of 2024
- Q3 2025 saw 160 deliveries -- the most since 2018

He also tracked quality metrics: a 75% reduction in "traveled work" (work done out of normal production sequence) on the 737 line, and 60% across all programs.

---

### Step 4: Qualitative Deep Dive -- Kelly Ortberg

> **Framework Step: [04-QUALITATIVE-DEEP-DIVE](04-QUALITATIVE-DEEP-DIVE.md)**

If there's one person this thesis depends on, it's CEO Kelly Ortberg.

> "I'm not pressuring the team to go fast. I'm pressuring the team to do it right." -- Kelly Ortberg

Kyler described him as "not a visionary but a mechanic" -- exactly what Boeing needed. His track record at Rockwell Collins was exceptional:
- Nearly doubled revenue from $4.6B to ~$8-9B
- Delivered ~20% operating margins consistently
- EPS rose every year he was in charge
- Stock more than doubled, outperforming the S&P 500
- 89% employee approval rating, named one of Glassdoor's Top 100 CEOs
- Business ultimately acquired for $30 billion

Since taking over at Boeing, Ortberg has:
- Resolved a machinist strike with a 38% wage increase over four years
- Raised $24.3 billion in equity to stabilize the balance sheet
- Reacquired Spirit AeroSystems to restore control over quality
- Pushed a cultural reset, encouraging employees to flag safety concerns
- Secured two key FAA approvals to increase production

> **Key Idea:** Kyler always keeps Charlie Munger's quote in mind: "Show me the incentives, and I will show you the outcome." [See 04-QUALITATIVE-DEEP-DIVE](04-QUALITATIVE-DEEP-DIVE.md) for why management quality matters so much.

---

### Step 5: Valuation

> **Framework Step: [05-VALUATION](05-VALUATION.md)**

| Component | Estimate | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| 2027 deliveries | ~840 planes | Full ramp of 737 (52/mo), 787 (10/mo), plus others |
| Revenue per plane | $74M | Up from $69M in Q3 2025, based on historical range |
| Commercial revenue | $62.16B | 840 x $74M |
| Defense & Space revenue | $28B | Mid-single-digit growth |
| Global Services revenue | $22.4B | Mid-single-digit growth, minus $2B divested |
| Spirit AeroSystems | $5.4B | 15% increase from acquisition levels |
| **Total 2027 Revenue** | **$117.96B** | |
| FCF Margin | 8% | Below 2018 peak of 13%, reflecting higher capex |
| **Free Cash Flow** | **$9.4B** | |
| Multiple | 26x | Wide-moat duopoly with margin expansion ahead |
| Share count | 760M | Some dilution from Spirit deal |
| **2027 Share Price** | **$323** | |
| CAGR from $212 | ~21% | Over 2.17 years |

Kyler noted this was below his usual 30% threshold for a new position, but well above the 10-15% level where he'd consider trimming. He viewed Boeing as a hold, not a fresh buy at $212.

---

### Step 6: The Decision

> **Framework Step: [06-THE-DECISION](06-THE-DECISION.md)**

Boeing passed Kyler's scoring system but with a wrinkle: the forward returns from $212 didn't meet the 30% hurdle for a new position. This is the framework working as designed -- it prevented Kyler from adding more at a price where the risk/reward had shifted.

He held his existing position with confidence, noting: "By year end 2026 their future might be brighter than I expected and we will need to extend the timeline and raise the outlook."

## Example 3: Alibaba ($BABA) -- The China Discount

**What it is:** The largest digital commerce platform in Asia, with 800 million+ users, the #1 cloud infrastructure provider in China, and one of the most complete AI pipelines in the East.

**When Kyler first covered it:** August 2024, at around $80/share.

**Price target:** $369 by 2029 (updated from original $375)

---

### Step 1: Finding the Idea

> **Framework Step: [01-FINDING-IDEAS](01-FINDING-IDEAS.md)** Alibaba was a textbook case of Kyler's approach: a massive, high-quality company that the market was treating like a value trap.

The numbers told a stark story. From 2016 to 2025, Alibaba's revenue grew more than 6x. The share price over that same window? Flat. The stock was down about 70% from its peak.

What had happened wasn't a business collapse -- it was a narrative collapse:
- China's government cracked down on big tech (2021-2022), blocking Ant Group's IPO and fining Alibaba billions
- US-China tensions escalated with tariffs and chip export controls
- Investors fled anything connected to China

Kyler recognized this as exactly the pattern he looks for: "Mispriced, unloved, and no one paying attention."

---

### Step 2: Running the First Filter

> **Framework Step: [02-FIRST-FILTER](02-FIRST-FILTER.md)**

**Solvency:** The opposite of a concern. Alibaba had $25.81 billion in net cash. This wasn't a company struggling to survive -- it was sitting on a fortress of a balance sheet.

**Dilution:** Actually the reverse -- Alibaba was buying back its own shares aggressively, shrinking the share count by roughly 3% per year. Management targeted continued buybacks.

**Revenue growth:** Even with headwinds, revenue continued to grow. Multiple segments (cloud, international commerce, logistics) showed strong momentum.

**ROCE/ROIC:** Strong historically and improving as the company shed unprofitable businesses and refocused.

**Rough valuation:** Trading at ~15x forward earnings for a company that generated $43 billion in free cash flow. Kyler called this "the same multiple you'd expect for a low growth, no-moat retailer" -- not a tech giant with dominant market positions.

---

### Step 3: Deep Analysis -- Understanding the Beast

> **Framework Step: [03-DEEP-ANALYSIS](03-DEEP-ANALYSIS.md)**

Alibaba is complex because it's actually many businesses:
- **China Retail** (Taobao + Tmall): The core commerce engine, plus newly integrated instant delivery (Ele.me)
- **Alibaba Cloud**: #1 in China with ~33% market share, AI revenue growing triple digits for 8 consecutive quarters
- **International Commerce** (AliExpress, Lazada, Trendyol): Approaching breakeven
- **Cainiao** (logistics): Spanning 200+ countries with 1,100+ warehouses
- **Ant Group stake** (Alipay): Worth tens of billions, for which Alibaba gets zero credit in its stock price

Kyler compared Alibaba to its competitors (PDD, JD, Meituan) on margins, growth, market share, and capital position. Alibaba was "the best capitalized" of the bunch.

---

### Step 4: Qualitative Deep Dive

> **Framework Step: [04-QUALITATIVE-DEEP-DIVE](04-QUALITATIVE-DEEP-DIVE.md)**

**Moat:** Dominant positions in multiple categories -- #1 in Chinese e-commerce (~40% of online retail), #1 in Chinese cloud (33%), and rapidly growing in instant commerce. The ecosystem is deeply integrated: shopping, payments (Alipay), logistics, and cloud all feed each other.

**Management:** Eddie Wu and Joe Tsai were refocusing the company on its core strengths, shedding non-essential businesses, and leaning into government alignment. Stock-based compensation was down more than 40% year-over-year -- management was paying cash bonuses instead of handing out equity, preserving shareholder ownership.

**The China Risk -- and why Kyler accepted it:** This is where the qualitative work matters most. Kyler didn't ignore the risk of investing in China. He accepted it because:
- The government had shifted from punishing tech to partnering with it
- China was explicitly targeting consumption growth (raising it from 52% to 70% of GDP by 2035)
- Demographics were favorable: a large wave of Chinese citizens entering the 35-44 peak spending bracket
- The margin of safety was enormous. Even accounting for "China risk," Alibaba was priced so cheaply that the math still worked

> **Key Idea:** "In a country where policy direction *is* capital allocation, alignment with the state is edge. Right now, Alibaba has it." -- Kyler

**Catalysts:**
- Massive AI investment (380 billion RMB planned for cloud + AI)
- Instant commerce integration driving daily engagement (MAUs up 200%+ since April 2025)
- Apple selecting Alibaba's Qwen AI model for iPhone features in China
- Government consumption stimulus creating a tailwind

---

### Step 5: Valuation

> **Framework Step: [05-VALUATION](05-VALUATION.md)**

Kyler projected out to 2029 because Alibaba's turnaround is multi-year:

| Component | Estimate | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| 2029 Net Revenue | 1.533 trillion RMB (~$214B) | 7.5% elimination for intercompany transactions |
| FCF Margin | 20% | Below 2023 peaks, well below pre-COVID levels |
| Free Cash Flow | 306.6B RMB (~$42.9B) | |
| Share count (ADRs) | ~2.12B | ~12% shrinkage from buybacks |
| FCF per share | $20.24 | |
| Multiple | 18x | Conservative for a company of this quality |
| **2029 Share Price** | **~$364-369** | |
| Upside from ~$80 entry | ~370% | |
| CAGR | ~25-35% | Depending on timeline |

> **Key Idea:** Kyler noted that this valuation was "before you factor in dividends, AI optionality, or a re-rating if sentiment around China and Alibaba starts to normalize." The actual upside could be much higher if the market simply starts treating Alibaba like other global tech platforms.

---

### Step 6: The Decision

> **Framework Step: [06-THE-DECISION](06-THE-DECISION.md)**

Alibaba became a core position in Kyler's portfolio. He bought heavily at prices below $100. By January 2026, BABA was up 15.68% in just that month, and Kyler noted he would no longer add to the position because forward returns from the higher price fell below his 30% threshold.

This illustrates an important principle: **the same company can be a "buy" at one price and a "hold" at another.** The framework forces that discipline.

## Example 4: Hormel ($HRL) -- The Boring Staples Play

**What it is:** A 135-year-old branded food company behind over 40 recognizable brands including SPAM, Skippy, Hormel Black Label, Planters, Jennie-O, and Applegate.

**When Kyler initiated the position:** January 2026, at around $24.20/share.

**Price target:** $50 by 2028 (~$54 including dividends)

---

### Step 1: Finding the Idea

> **Framework Step: [01-FINDING-IDEAS](01-FINDING-IDEAS.md)** Another credit to Thomas J. Hayes for bringing this to Kyler's attention.

Hormel was down 55% from its highs, trading at its lowest levels since 2009. For a company with more than a century of operations, 40+ household brands, and a 60-year dividend growth streak, that's the kind of extreme discount that triggers Kyler's radar.

This wasn't a broken business. It was a boring business going through a temporary storm of high input costs (pork, beef, and nuts all spiking), an avian flu hitting turkey production, and operational disruptions at a key Planters facility.

---

### Step 2: Running the First Filter

> **Framework Step: [02-FIRST-FILTER](02-FIRST-FILTER.md)**

**Solvency:** Rock solid. Hormel ended fiscal 2025 with:
- $671 million in cash
- $750 million available via revolving credit facility
- Staggered debt maturities: $500M (2027), $750M (2028), $1B (2030), $600M (2051)
- Interest coverage ratio of 9.2x
- Net debt to EBITDA of 2.2x

Kyler's conclusion: management has the runway to focus on improving profitability rather than managing debt.

**Dilution:** Not a concern -- stable share count.

**Revenue growth:** Modest but positive. Four consecutive quarters of organic growth (5.7% in Q3 2025, 2.3% in Q4). Management guided for 1-4% growth in 2026.

**ROCE/ROIC:** Temporarily depressed by margin compression from input costs. Historical track record was strong.

**Rough valuation:** At $24.20, Hormel was priced as if margins would never recover. If they did -- even partially -- the stock had 100%+ upside. That cleared the 30% CAGR hurdle.

---

### Step 3: Deep Analysis -- Understanding the Margin Problem

> **Framework Step: [03-DEEP-ANALYSIS](03-DEEP-ANALYSIS.md)**

Hormel's problem was simple to understand but painful to live through. Input costs -- primarily pork (45% of inputs), beef (15%), and nuts -- had spiked faster than Hormel could raise prices.

The numbers were brutal:
- Beef prices up ~20% year-over-year
- Pork bellies up ~25%
- Pork cutout up ~10%
- Trim up ~20%
- Margin headwinds of 400 **basis points** (a basis point is 1/100th of 1% -- see [09-GLOSSARY.md](09-GLOSSARY.md)) in Q3 and 500 basis points in Q4

But Hormel wasn't sitting still. The "Transform & Modernize" initiative had already delivered ~$200 million in cumulative operating income benefit through 2024-2025. SKU rationalization, org simplification (9% of corporate and sales roles cut), and technology investments were all in motion.

Key insight: the cost-cutting payback periods were under 12 months, and gross savings were 2-3x implementation costs. These weren't one-time tricks -- they were repeatable.

---

### Step 4: Qualitative Deep Dive

> **Framework Step: [04-QUALITATIVE-DEEP-DIVE](04-QUALITATIVE-DEEP-DIVE.md)**

**Competitive advantages -- the power of brands:** In packaged food, without strong brands the business comes down to price -- a race to the bottom. With trusted brands, price competition doesn't disappear, but it becomes more flexible. Companies can raise prices over time without losing as much volume.

Hormel owns some of the most recognizable food brands in the country. That brand strength showed up in the data:
- Jennie-O lean ground turkey: growing faster than the category, gaining share, volume-driven even after price hikes
- Planters: distribution up ~13% year-over-year, ~12% growth in dollar consumption

**Catalysts:**
- Pricing catching up to input costs (the lag was the problem, not inability to price)
- Planters facility back online after disruption -- the most visible near-term catalyst
- Structural shift toward higher protein consumption (71% of Americans actively trying to eat more protein in 2024)
- GLP-1 medications creating additional protein demand (12% of US adults now using GLP-1 drugs, which increase the need for protein to preserve muscle)

> **Key Idea:** Kyler wasn't betting on a comeback from nowhere. He was betting that a 135-year-old company with dominant brands would do what it has always done: pass costs through and return to normal margins. It had done this through the 2008-2009 cycle and the COVID era.

---

### Step 5: Valuation

> **Framework Step: [05-VALUATION](05-VALUATION.md)**

| Component | Estimate | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| 2028 Revenue | ~$12.98B | From $12B base at 2-3% growth/year |
| FCF Margin | 8.4% | ~400 bps improvement from depressed 2025 levels |
| Free Cash Flow | ~$1.09B | |
| EV/FCF Multiple | 28x | 10-year median, supported by brand quality and ~4.8% dividend yield |
| Implied Market Cap | ~$27.66B | |
| **2028 Share Price** | **~$50.29** | |
| Plus dividends (~$3.56/share) | **~$53.85 total value** | |
| Upside from $24.20 | ~122% | |
| CAGR | ~31% | Through 2028 |

**Realistic downside case:** Hormel isn't a company that goes to zero. Even if the turnaround stalls, the business stays relatively stable. Kyler estimated ~$20/share as a realistic floor outside of a recession. That's only ~17% downside versus 122% upside.

---

### Step 6: The Decision

> **Framework Step: [06-THE-DECISION](06-THE-DECISION.md) and [07-POSITION-SIZING](07-POSITION-SIZING.md)**

Hormel scored well enough that Kyler made it a NEW POSITION in January 2026. He actually sold two existing positions (Valaris and FMC) to make room -- not because those theses had broken, but because Hormel was "a meaningfully better opportunity."

He sized it at up to 16% of his portfolio. That's a large position, reflecting the combination of limited downside, solid upside, and the quality of the underlying business.

> **Key Idea:** This is Kyler's opportunity cost framework in action [see 06-THE-DECISION](06-THE-DECISION.md)]. Every position in the portfolio has to earn its spot. When a better opportunity appears, weaker holdings get replaced. Capital isn't loyal -- it goes where it works hardest.

---

## What These Four Examples Have in Common

Despite being very different businesses in very different industries, all four investments share the same DNA:

1. **Quality businesses with temporary problems.** None of these companies were dying. All of them had real issues -- but issues that were fixable, not fatal.

2. **The market was pricing problems as permanent.** In each case, the stock price reflected fear rather than fundamentals. The market assumed the worst would last forever.

3. **Clear catalysts for change.** Auto sales recovering. Boeing ramping production. China's government turning pro-consumption. Input costs normalizing. Kyler never buys "cheap" alone -- there must be a reason for the situation to improve.

4. **Simple valuation math.** Revenue x FCF Margin x Multiple / Shares. No complex models. The idea should be obvious enough that you don't need fancy math to see it.

5. **Discipline in sizing and decision-making.** The scoring system forced Kyler to compare every new idea against existing holdings. No position was sacred. Capital flowed to the best opportunities.

> "An investable idea should be obvious. This final step forces me to walk through the math and confirm the numbers actually make sense." -- Kyler
