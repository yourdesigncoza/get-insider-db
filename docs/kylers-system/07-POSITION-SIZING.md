# 07 - Position Sizing: How Much to Bet

*Kyler's rules for deciding how much money goes into each stock, and how he manages risk across the portfolio.*

---

You have found a great company. You have scored it, compared it to your other options, and decided it deserves a spot in the portfolio. But now comes a question that separates good investors from reckless ones:

**How much of your money should you put into it?**

Bet too little on your best ideas and you waste your edge. Bet too much on any single idea and one bad outcome can wreck everything. **Position sizing** (see [Glossary](09-GLOSSARY.md)) is the art of getting this right.

---

## The Analogy: Filling Your Backpack for a Hike

Imagine you are packing a backpack for a 3-day hike. You have limited space and weight capacity. You need water (essential), food (important), a first-aid kit (just in case), and a camera (nice to have).

If you fill 80% of the backpack with camera equipment because you love photography, you will not have enough room for water and food. If something goes wrong, you are in trouble.

Smart packing means giving the most space to the most important items while keeping room for emergencies. That is exactly how Kyler thinks about portfolio allocation.

## Score-Based Position Sizing (Current System)

> **Key Idea:** In Kyler's current system, the final score from his 3 weighted inputs ([see 06-THE-DECISION.md](06-THE-DECISION.md)) directly dictates the maximum position size. Higher score = more of his money can go into that stock.

Kyler describes this with remarkable simplicity: *"I genuinely keep it this simple, which is why I'm willing to spend more than an hour, even after all the research, thinking carefully about the score I assign to each stock."*

He published a chart showing score ranges mapped to maximum position sizes. The relationship is straightforward: a stock with a high score (low downside, high probability of success, strong returns) gets a larger allocation than one with a lower score.

This system was introduced in December 2025 to replace the earlier conviction matrix.

### The 3 Metrics That Drive Position Size

The position size comes from the same three inputs used in the scoring system, but with slightly different weights when focused on sizing:

1. **Estimated % downside from current levels (worst case)** -- 50% weight
2. **Estimated probability base case happens** -- 35% weight
3. **CAGR from base case scenario** -- 15% weight

Notice that downside risk carries *even more* weight here than in the scoring decision (50% vs 45%). When it comes to how much money you actually put at risk, Kyler shifts the emphasis further toward protecting capital.

### Hard Breakpoints

Kyler built automatic cutoffs into the system. If any of these conditions are true, the system outputs a position size of zero -- meaning he will not invest regardless of how attractive other factors look:

| Condition | Result |
|---|---|
| Expected CAGR below 30% | Position size = 0% |
| Probability of base case below 60% | Position size = 0% |
| Downside risk of 80-99% | Position capped at 5% |
| Downside risk of 100% (total loss possible) | Position capped at 3% |

**Breakpoints** (see [Glossary](09-GLOSSARY.md)) are automatic rules that override everything else. They exist because even a great opportunity is not worth taking if the potential loss is too large or the expected return is too low.

> **Key Idea:** The breakpoints tell you a lot about Kyler's priorities. He would rather miss a potentially amazing stock with 25% CAGR than own one that does not clear his 30% hurdle. And he would rather miss a high-return stock with only 55% probability than bet on something that is nearly a coin flip.

Kyler explains the philosophy behind these weights: *"I have built the weightings to disproportionately favor low downside risk opportunities. This is to follow Buffett's #1 rule. Don't lose money."*

## The Earlier System: The Conviction Matrix (Historical Context)

Before the score-based system, Kyler used a simpler matrix that mapped **conviction** and **expected CAGR** to position sizes. Understanding this helps you see how the system evolved.

### The May 2025 Matrix

The original matrix used two axes:

**Conviction levels** (based on how much needed to go right):
- Low: Many unknowns or risks
- Medium: 2-3 key things need to play out
- High: Odds heavily in your favor

**Expected return (CAGR) ranges:**
- 30-40%
- 40-60%
- 60-80%
- 80%+

Position sizes ranged from as low as 2-5% (low conviction, lower returns) up to 10-15% (high conviction, 80%+ CAGR). Starting weights were smaller, with the option to scale up if the stock price fell and conviction held.

### The July 2025 Matrix

The mid-2025 version refined the conviction and risk definitions:

**Conviction:**
- Medium: 60%+ probability the thesis plays out
- Medium/High: 80%+ probability
- High: 90%+ probability

**Risk (Reasonable Worst Case):**
- High: Sizable losses possible, or 100% loss risk
- Moderate: 30-50% potential losses
- Low: Less than 30% potential losses

### Why Kyler Moved Away from the Matrix

The matrix had a limitation Kyler openly acknowledged: *"Previously my decision making framework had changed a couple times with inputs coming from expected returns, risk, and conviction but final results were largely guided by gut feel and instinct."*

The weighted scoring system solved this by producing a single number. No more subjective judgment calls about where a stock falls in a 2D grid. The number either says "invest X%" or it does not.

## Risk Management Rules

Position sizing is only half of risk management. The other half is a set of hard rules that apply to the entire portfolio.

### Max Positions: 12

Kyler caps his portfolio at 12 stocks. He explains: *"I do not have infinite bandwidth and want to maintain a concentrated portfolio."*

A **concentrated portfolio** (see [Glossary](09-GLOSSARY.md)) means owning fewer stocks with larger positions in each. The opposite approach -- owning 50 or 100 stocks -- is called **diversification**. Kyler believes that if you do the work to find truly great opportunities, spreading your money across 50 stocks dilutes your edge.

Think of it like a basketball team. You only get 5 players on the court. You want your best 5, not your best 12 -- and definitely not all 50 people who tried out.

### Correlation Rule: Never More Than 50% in One Catalyst

Kyler writes: *"I never want more than 50% of the portfolio tied to a single catalyst, and in many cases that limit is lower depending on the industry."*

**Correlation** (see [Glossary](09-GLOSSARY.md)) means how much different investments move together. If you own three natural gas companies and gas prices crash, all three get crushed at once.

Kyler manages this by understanding the **key drivers** behind each position. Two stocks might be in different industries but still be correlated -- for example, a shipping company and an oil driller both depend on global trade activity.

He acknowledges this is still a work in progress: *"This is an area where I'm still refining my thinking and expect the rules to evolve."*

## Leverage: Borrowing Money to Invest

### What Leverage Is

**Leverage** (see [Glossary](09-GLOSSARY.md)) means borrowing money from your broker to invest more than you actually have. If you have $10,000 and use 15% leverage, you are investing $11,500 -- your $10,000 plus $1,500 borrowed.

Think of leverage like a magnifying glass held over a piece of paper in the sun. It concentrates the light and makes the hot spot brighter. But that same magnification works both ways -- if the paper moves, the burn gets worse, not just a little worse, but proportionally worse.

When stocks go up, leverage makes your gains bigger. When stocks go down, leverage makes your losses bigger. And if losses get large enough, your broker can force you to sell -- called a **margin call** (see [Glossary](09-GLOSSARY.md)) -- at the worst possible time.

### Kyler's Leverage Rules

Kyler keeps leverage low by default and only increases it when markets crash (meaning prices are cheaper and risk is lower):

| S&P 500 Condition | Max Leverage Allowed |
|---|---|
| Normal markets | 15% |
| S&P 500 drops 10% | 20% |
| S&P 500 drops 15% | 25% |
| S&P 500 drops 25% | 30% |

His January 2026 portfolio adds up to about 115% of his capital, meaning he was using roughly 15% leverage at the time.

Kyler explains the logic: *"I've found these levels leave enough room to withstand even severe market corrections while staying comfortably clear of liquidity constraints. I may adjust these levels lower over time."*

> **Key Idea:** The scaling system is counterintuitive but smart. Most people use MORE leverage when markets are going up (because they feel confident) and LESS when markets crash (because they are scared). Kyler does the opposite. When markets crash, stocks are cheaper, so the risk of further decline is lower, which means leverage is safer. When markets are high, stocks are expensive, so leverage is more dangerous.

He also keeps emergency capital on the sidelines for extreme situations: *"In practice, this would only apply during drawdowns of 50% or more, at which point I would already be preparing to deploy most available capital."*

One quote he always keeps in mind:

> *"Markets can remain irrational longer than you can remain solvent."* -- John Maynard Keynes

This means that even if you are right about a stock being undervalued, the market can keep pushing it lower for longer than you can survive financially. Leverage amplifies this danger.

Kyler also considers the macro environment before adding leverage: *"If those trends are clearly negative, I may choose not to increase leverage even during market declines."* This means even the leverage scaling table is not automatic -- fundamental context matters.

## Options: The Strategy Kyler Stopped Using

**Options** (see [Glossary](09-GLOSSARY.md)) are contracts that give you the right to buy a stock at a certain price by a certain date. They can multiply your returns -- but they can also go to zero, meaning you lose every dollar you put in.

### How Kyler Used Them (2024-2025)

During 2024 and most of 2025, options were a significant part of Kyler's strategy. His rules were strict:

- **Only long-dated calls**: At least 12 months until expiration. This avoided the timing trap where you are right about the stock but wrong about when it moves.
- **Max 15% of portfolio in options**: Because options can go to zero, Kyler limited total exposure.
- **Must be liquid**: The options needed trading volume and tight **bid-ask spreads** (see [Glossary](09-GLOSSARY.md)) -- meaning you could buy and sell them without getting ripped off by wide price gaps.
- **3x intrinsic value rule**: Kyler wanted high confidence the stock would reach at least 3 times his option cost above the strike price at expiration. For example, a $5 option with a $50 strike meant he wanted the stock to hit at least $65.
- **Reasonable implied volatility**: Options are more expensive when the market expects big moves (**implied volatility** -- see [Glossary](09-GLOSSARY.md)). Kyler looked to buy when implied volatility was average or lower.

### Why He Stopped

In late 2025, Kyler stopped using options entirely. The reason was refreshingly honest:

> *"Not because the results were poor, but because my framework and understanding in this area are lacking. As with any investment, I need a very clear understanding of what I'm doing and what I expect to happen. With options, despite having a decent base of knowledge, I was too careless. That carelessness showed up in my two most recent positions, where I lost a meaningful amount of capital, even though overall I've still made far more than I've lost."*

He has not ruled out returning to options in the future: *"Potentially in late 2026 or early 2027, I may dedicate time to improving my knowledge and skill set in this area."*

> **Key Idea:** Knowing when you are not good enough at something to use it responsibly is itself a skill. Kyler made money with options overall but recognized that his process was not rigorous enough. Rather than keep getting lucky, he stopped until he can do it properly. This is circle of competence in action.

## Real-World Example: Sizing in the Jan 2026 Portfolio

Kyler's January 2026 portfolio update shows his system in action. His portfolio added up to about 115% of capital (15% leverage), spread across 10 positions.

Some positions were well above the sizes his current scoring system would suggest. Kyler explains why: *"You may notice some positions are well above the position sizing I lay out. This is due to the risk reward shifting over time. The position sizing information provided is based on current levels. For example I was buying BABA below $100 where the downside risk was far lower and potential returns higher."*

Alibaba had a position sizing of 0% at current prices -- meaning Kyler would not buy more at those levels -- but he still held his large existing position because the stock had risen since his purchases.

This illustrates an important principle: **position sizing tells you what to do with NEW money.** It does not automatically force you to sell existing positions that have grown beyond their target size. Selling decisions follow the rules in [08-AFTER-THE-BUY.md](08-AFTER-THE-BUY.md).

## The Bottom Line

Position sizing is where discipline either holds or breaks. It is easy to fall in love with an idea and put 25% of your money into it. Kyler's system prevents that by tying size directly to a mathematical score, capped by hard rules.

The key principles:

- **Score determines size.** Higher score = larger allowed position.
- **Downside dominates.** The system heavily favors stocks where you can't lose much.
- **Hard breakpoints exist.** Below 30% CAGR or below 60% probability = no investment.
- **12 positions max.** Concentrated bets on your best ideas.
- **Leverage is earned.** Only increases when prices drop, never when confidence rises.
- **Know your limits.** Kyler stopped using options because his process was not good enough, even though he was making money.
