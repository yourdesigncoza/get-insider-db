# 06 - The Decision: Should I Invest?

*Kyler's scoring system for comparing opportunities and making the final call.*

---

You have done days of research. You know the company inside and out -- the financials, the management, the catalysts, the risks. You have built a valuation model and believe the stock can return 30%+ per year.

But here is the question most investors get wrong: **should you actually buy it?**

Having a great stock idea is not the same as making a great investment decision. The decision depends on what else you could do with that money -- and how much risk you are taking on.

This chapter covers Kyler's system for answering that question.

---

## The Analogy: Choosing Which Store to Open

Imagine you have saved up $10,000, and you are deciding which small business to open.

- **Store A** sells phone cases. You think there is a 90% chance it succeeds and could earn you $5,000/year. But if it fails, you only lose $2,000 because you can sell the leftover inventory.
- **Store B** sells custom sneakers. You think there is a 70% chance it succeeds and could earn you $8,000/year. But if it fails, you lose $7,000 because custom sneakers are hard to resell.

Which is better? Store B makes more money -- but Store A is much safer. How do you compare them fairly?

Kyler built a scoring system to answer exactly this kind of question for stocks.

---

## The 3 Weighted Inputs

> **Key Idea:** Kyler's decision system boils down every stock to three numbers, each weighted by importance. The result is a single score he can use to compare any opportunity to any other.

After completing his full research process ([see 05-VALUATION.md](05-VALUATION.md)), Kyler estimates three things for every stock:

### 1. Reasonable Worst Case Scenario (45% Weight)

This is the single most important input -- it gets the heaviest weight.

After all that research, Kyler asks: **"What happens if things go wrong? How much money could I lose?"**

He quantifies this as a percentage decline in share price. For example: "If my thesis is wrong, I estimate the stock could fall 30% from here."

**Downside risk** (see [Glossary](09-GLOSSARY.md)) means how much money you could lose if things do not go as planned.

> **Key Idea:** Downside risk is treated on a curve. Moving from 20% downside to 30% downside carries roughly 1.5x the penalty that the raw numbers suggest. This means the system punishes high-risk stocks more aggressively. A stock with 60% potential downside is not just "twice as bad" as one with 30% downside -- it is dramatically worse in the scoring.

Think of it like speed limits. Going from 30 mph to 40 mph is a small increase in danger. Going from 80 mph to 90 mph is a much bigger increase, even though it is the same 10 mph difference. Risk works the same way -- the higher it gets, the faster it compounds.

This follows Warren Buffett's rule #1: **Don't lose money.**

### 2. Base Case Probability (40% Weight)

This answers: **"How likely is it that my base case valuation -- or better -- actually plays out?"**

The **base case** (see [Glossary](09-GLOSSARY.md)) is the scenario Kyler built in his valuation model -- the revenue growth, margin improvement, and multiple he believes are reasonable. This input estimates the probability that reality meets or exceeds that scenario.

For example: "I believe there is a 75% chance this company hits my base case targets, based on the quality of the business and management."

A well-run company in a stable industry with clear catalysts might get 80-90%. A turnaround with shaky management in a volatile industry might get 60-65%.

### 3. Base Case CAGR (15% Weight)

This answers: **"What annual return does my base case imply from today's price?"**

**CAGR** stands for **Compound Annual Growth Rate** (see [Glossary](09-GLOSSARY.md)) -- it is the average yearly return if the stock hits Kyler's target price in the expected time frame.

This gets the lowest weight because Kyler's hurdle rate already filters for 30%+ CAGR. By the time a stock reaches this stage, CAGR has already passed a high bar. The differences between stocks are smaller here compared to the first two inputs.

---

## How the Score Works

These three inputs, with their weights (45% / 40% / 15%), combine into a single numerical score. A higher score means a better risk-adjusted opportunity.

Here is a simplified example showing how two stocks might compare:

| Input | Stock A | Stock B |
|---|---|---|
| Worst Case Downside | 25% loss | 50% loss |
| Base Case Probability | 80% | 70% |
| Base Case CAGR | 40%/yr | 55%/yr |

Stock B has a higher potential return, but Stock A has much less downside and a higher probability of success. In Kyler's system, Stock A would likely score higher because the system heavily weights safety (45%) and probability (40%).

> **Key Idea:** Kyler is fully transparent about the limitations of this system. He writes: *"I'm fully aware these figures are abstract. I have no way to prove any of them are precisely correct, and I know they are all wrong to some degree. Even after an investment plays out, I won't know whether I quantified these inputs correctly, regardless of the outcome."* The point is not precision -- it is **consistency**. Using the same framework every time prevents emotional decisions.

## The 3 Decision Scenarios

Having a score is not enough. Kyler still has to decide what to DO with it. The answer depends on his current portfolio situation.

### Scenario 1: Cash Available + Want Exposure to the Theme

This is the simplest case. Kyler has money to invest and wants exposure to this particular industry or theme.

**What he does:** Compare the new idea's score to his best existing position that still has room to add more shares. Whichever scores higher gets the capital.

- If the new idea scores higher, he takes the new position.
- If the existing position scores higher, he adds to what he already owns.
- Once that position is full, he repeats the comparison with the next-best opportunity.

Think of it like a sports draft. You pick the best available player. If you already have a great quarterback and the new one is not better, you draft at a different position instead.

### Scenario 2: No Cash + Want Exposure

This is harder. Kyler has no spare cash, so buying something new means selling something he already owns.

**What he does:** Compare the new idea to his least attractive existing holding. It must be *clearly* better.

He checks two things in order:

1. **Does any current position have forward returns below 15% per year?** If yes, and the new position has lower downside risk, he will sell the low-return stock for the new idea.

2. **If no position has forward returns that low**, he compares scores directly. The gap needs to be **40+ points** to justify selling the old position for the new one.

**Forward returns** (see [Glossary](09-GLOSSARY.md)) are the expected annual returns from the current price going forward. A stock Kyler bought at $20 that has risen to $80 might only have 12% forward returns from $80, even though his original thesis was for 50%+ CAGR from $20.

> **Key Idea:** The 40-point score gap is a high bar on purpose. Every trade has costs -- commissions, taxes, the risk of being wrong about the new idea. Kyler does not want to churn his portfolio by constantly swapping positions for marginal improvements.

### Scenario 3: No Cash + Already Maxed on Theme Exposure

This is the most restrictive case. Kyler has no cash AND his portfolio already has all the exposure he wants to this particular industry.

For example, if his portfolio already has 15% in energy stocks and he does not want more, he cannot just add another energy stock on top.

**What he does:** Compare the new idea to the weakest holding *within that same theme*. Then apply the same rules as Scenario 2.

This prevents the portfolio from becoming dangerously concentrated in one area.

## Opportunity Cost and Correlation Risk

Two concepts run through every decision Kyler makes:

**Opportunity cost** (see [Glossary](09-GLOSSARY.md)) means that every dollar you put into Stock A is a dollar you cannot put into Stock B. Buying a "good" stock can still be a bad decision if you passed up a "great" stock to do it.

Back to our store analogy: if you only have $10,000, opening the phone case store means you cannot open the sneaker store. The phone case store might be good, but if the sneaker store would have been better, you paid an invisible cost.

**Correlation risk** (see [Glossary](09-GLOSSARY.md)) means owning too many stocks that move in the same direction for the same reason. If you own five oil companies and oil prices crash, all five get hit at once.

Kyler writes: *"I never want more than 50% of the portfolio tied to a single catalyst."*

## How This System Evolved

Kyler's decision framework has changed significantly over time. Understanding the history helps you see how a real investor refines their process.

### Early Version (Mid-2025): The Conviction Matrix

The earlier system was simpler. Kyler used two axes -- **conviction level** and **risk level** -- to decide position size:

**Conviction Levels:**
- Medium: 60%+ probability the thesis plays out
- Medium/High: 80%+ probability
- High: 90%+ probability

**Risk Levels:**
- High: Sizable losses possible, or 100% loss possible
- Moderate: 30-50% potential losses
- Low: Less than 30% potential losses

Kyler was honest about its limitations: *"I want to be clear. I lay all of this out like these are firm and measurable. They are not. These measures are completely subjective based on my understanding and research on the company."*

### Current Version (Jan 2026): The 3-Input Weighted Score

The current system replaced the matrix with the three weighted inputs described above. The key improvement was **consistency** -- the weighted score gives a single number that can be compared across any two stocks, rather than relying on a matrix where "gut feel" often made the final call.

As Kyler explains: *"Previously my decision making framework had changed a couple times with inputs coming from expected returns, risk, and conviction but final results were largely guided by gut feel and instinct. I spent a few weeks thinking about this issue and studying how others approach it... In the end I came up with a model that makes my decision making far more consistent."*

## Real-World Context: Replacing FMC and Valaris

In January 2026, Kyler exited two positions -- Valaris (offshore drilling) and FMC (crop chemicals) -- to buy Hormel (food company).

He wrote: *"This was not due to any changes in the thesis for either company but rather Hormel being a meaningfully better opportunity."*

This is Scenario 2 in action. Hormel scored high enough to clear the bar over existing holdings. The decision was not emotional -- it came from running the same three inputs through the same scoring system and finding that Hormel offered better risk-adjusted returns at current prices.

---

## The Bottom Line

The decision system exists for one reason: **to remove emotion from the final call.**

After all the research is done, your brain wants to buy because you have spent so much time on it. Kyler's system forces you to prove that the new idea is actually better than what you already have, with a high enough margin to justify the trade.

> **Key Idea:** The score is the starting point, not the ending point. It tells Kyler which opportunity deserves capital. The *amount* of capital it gets is determined by position sizing rules covered in the next chapter.
