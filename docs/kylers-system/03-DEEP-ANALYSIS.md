# Going Deeper: Business Cycles, Margins, and Competition

*Step 3 of Kyler's system. You've found a company that passed the basic filters. Now it's time to compare it against its rivals and decide if it truly deserves your time.*

---

## Why This Step Exists

Imagine you're picking players for a basketball team. You wouldn't just look at one player's stats in isolation -- you'd compare them to the other options. Who scores more? Who plays better defense? Who stays healthy?

That's exactly what Kyler does here. After a company passes the basic financial checks ([see 02-FIRST-FILTER.md](02-FIRST-FILTER.md)), he lines it up against competitors in the same industry. The goal: find the company that clearly stands out as the best quality in the group.

> **Key Idea:** This step is about *relative* quality. A company might look fine on its own, but if three competitors are better on every metric, it gets cut.

---

## What Kyler Compares

When Kyler has a shortlist of companies in the same industry, he compares them across several dimensions. Here's what he's looking at and why each matters.

### 1. Business Cycle History

A **business cycle** is the natural rise and fall of economic activity over time. Think of it like seasons -- businesses have good years and bad years, and this pattern tends to repeat.

Kyler wants to know:

- **How has the company performed during past downturns?** Did revenue collapse, or just dip?
- **Did it bounce back?** Some companies recover quickly. Others never fully recover.
- **How does the current trough compare to past troughs?** If the current downturn looks similar to previous ones the company survived, that's reassuring.

**Why it matters:** If a company has bounced back from tough times before, there's a reasonable chance it can do it again. If this downturn is *worse* than anything it's faced before, that's a warning sign.

**Real example -- Cooper Standard (CPS):** Cooper Standard's **ROIC** (Return on Invested Capital -- a measure of how efficiently a company uses money to generate profits; [see Glossary](09-GLOSSARY.md)) has a 20-year median of about 8.5%. That's below Kyler's usual 10% preference. But when the auto industry cycles up, CPS often hits the mid-teens or higher. Kyler understood this cyclical pattern and kept CPS on the list because the current trough looked like previous recoverable downturns -- not a permanent decline.

**Real example -- Hormel (HRL):** Hormel has been making food for over 135 years. During the 2008-2009 financial crisis, the stock got this cheap (relative to its own history) and eventually recovered. During the COVID era, margins got squeezed by commodity costs but recovered over time. Kyler sees the current downturn as following the same pattern, which is why he compares today's trough to those prior cycles.

### 2. Margin Trends

**Margins** tell you how much profit a company keeps from each dollar of revenue (see [Glossary](09-GLOSSARY.md)). If a company earns $100 in revenue and keeps $10 as profit, that's a 10% profit margin.

Kyler looks for one specific danger signal:

> **Key Idea:** "Any clear downtrend in margins indicates eroding advantages or pricing power. If I spot any worrying signs here, it is very likely this goes into a **permanent pass list**."

A **permanent pass list** is Kyler's way of saying "I won't invest in this company unless something fundamentally changes." Despite the name, companies *can* come off this list -- but only if new management fundamentally changes the business.

**What "eroding margins" looks like:**
- Margins that have been falling steadily for 5+ years
- A company that used to keep 15 cents of every dollar but now keeps only 8 cents
- Competitors maintaining their margins while this company's are shrinking

**Why it matters:** Falling margins usually mean the company is losing its competitive edge. Maybe competitors are undercutting their prices. Maybe customers are switching to cheaper alternatives. Whatever the cause, it's a sign the business is getting weaker, not stronger.

**How Kyler uses this with Hormel:** Hormel's margins were "heavily pressured by rapid input cost inflation that outpaced pricing." But Kyler distinguished between *temporarily depressed margins* (caused by a lag between rising costs and price increases) versus *structurally declining margins* (caused by the business getting worse). The key difference: Hormel was actively taking price increases and cutting costs. The margin squeeze was a timing issue, not a permanent problem.

### 3. Industry Pressures: Headwinds vs. Tailwinds

A **headwind** is something working against a company. A **tailwind** is something helping it. Think of wind when you're riding a bike -- headwinds slow you down, tailwinds speed you up.

Kyler needs to understand:

- **What forces are pushing the industry forward or holding it back?**
- **Are these forces temporary or permanent?**

**Temporary headwinds (usually OK):**
- A one-time supply chain disruption
- A short-term spike in raw material costs
- A single bad quarter of consumer spending

**Permanent headwinds (dangerous):**
- An entire industry being replaced by new technology
- Long-term demographic shifts away from the product
- Regulations that permanently raise costs with no offset

**Real example -- GLP-1 drugs and protein:** Kyler notes that GLP-1 weight-loss drugs (like Ozempic) are actually *increasing* demand for protein. Medical research shows that people on these drugs need more protein to avoid losing muscle mass. This is a tailwind for Hormel, which is heavily weighted toward protein products. Kyler specifically looks for the company "most heavily weighted toward protein" within the food industry.

**Real example -- Boeing:** Boeing faces temporary headwinds (production caps from the FAA, quality rework from past mistakes) and permanent tailwinds (only two companies in the world make large commercial aircraft, and the backlog is $636 billion). Kyler weighs the temporary nature of the problems against the durable nature of the advantages.

### 4. Competitor Comparison

This is the heart of the step. Kyler lines up companies side by side and compares:

| What to Compare | Why It Matters |
|---|---|
| **Leverage** (how much debt) | A company drowning in debt may not survive long enough for the turnaround |
| **Returns on capital** (ROIC/ROCE) | Shows how efficiently the business uses money |
| **Margins** | Higher margins = more profit from each sale |
| **Industry niches** | Is the company in the best position within the industry? |
| **Growth** | Is revenue expanding or shrinking? |
| **Dilution** | Is the company issuing shares and watering down your ownership? |
| **Potential returns** | What's the upside from current prices? |

> **Key Idea:** The goal is to identify "the company that clearly stands out as the highest quality within the group. It's not always possible or obvious, but that is the goal."

## How Kyler Defines "Quality"

This is one of the most important concepts in the entire system. "Quality" isn't just one thing -- it's a weighted combination of factors.

### Balance Sheet Strength Comes First

Kyler is crystal clear about this:

> "Quality, for me today, is weighted heavily toward balance sheet strength. Especially in turnarounds, this is often the deciding factor. No matter how good returns on capital or growth potential may be, **if the company doesn't have the time to make it happen, none of it matters.**"

A **balance sheet** is like a snapshot of everything a company owns and everything it owes (see [Glossary](09-GLOSSARY.md)). **Balance sheet strength** means the company has enough cash, low enough debt, and enough breathing room to survive a rough patch.

**Why this matters so much for turnarounds:** Turnaround companies are, by definition, going through a difficult period. If they run out of money before things improve, the stock goes to zero. It doesn't matter how great the business *could* be -- survival comes first.

**Real example -- Cooper Standard:** In 2021, CPS burned through $211 million in cash, had only $248 million left, and owed $1 billion in debt with $630 million due within three years. Many thought they'd go bankrupt. But management refinanced the debt (pushed payments out further), cut costs aggressively ($56 million saved in 2023 alone), and generated positive free cash flow. Kyler tracked whether CPS had enough runway to survive. The answer was yes -- barely -- and that's what made it investable.

**Real example -- Boeing:** After its crises, Boeing raised $24.3 billion in fresh capital, ended up with $23 billion in cash plus $10 billion from a business sale and $10 billion in undrawn credit lines -- all against $53.4 billion in debt with the first maturity not until 2030. Kyler's conclusion: "Liquidity is not a concern anymore."

### Then: Superior Niches or Margins

After balance sheet strength, Kyler looks for something that makes the company *better positioned* than its peers:

- **In commodity industries** (where products are basically the same), he looks for **superior margins**. If everyone sells the same stuff, the company that can produce it cheaper wins.
- **In industries where brands or positioning matter**, he looks for **the company most directly exposed to the relevant catalyst or trend.**

**Real example -- Hormel and protein:** In packaged food, Kyler looked for the company "most heavily weighted toward protein" because of the GLP-1 tailwind. Hormel, with brands like Jennie-O turkey, Hormel pepperoni, and Planters nuts, fit that description.

### Quality Is Evaluated as a Whole

Kyler adds an important nuance:

> "Quality has to be evaluated as a whole. With weight given to risk, certainty, and potential returns. Just because one company carries the lowest risk doesn't mean it automatically wins the comparison."

A company with slightly more risk but vastly higher potential returns might be the better choice. It's a balancing act.

---

## When Weaker Companies Stay on the List

Not every company that falls behind its peers gets immediately removed. Kyler keeps weaker companies on the list if they meet *all three* of these conditions:

1. **A deeper review doesn't reveal a failure in an earlier step** that was previously missed
2. **They offer materially higher potential returns** than the stronger peers
3. **There aren't many alternative opportunities** at the time

> **Key Idea:** "This doesn't mean I invest in them or even research them further. It simply means I keep them available in case stronger ideas don't pan out after deeper investigation."

Think of it like keeping a backup plan. The best candidate might not work out after deeper research (Step 4), so having alternatives ready saves time.

## Real Examples: How Kyler Compares Businesses

### Boeing vs. Airbus (Capital Requirements Moat)

When Kyler analyzed Boeing, the competitor comparison was unusually simple: there are only two companies in the world that make large commercial aircraft. COMAC (China) hasn't received regulatory approval outside China. Embraer focuses on smaller regional planes and has "no near-term ambition" to compete.

That left Boeing and Airbus as the only real choices. If one stumbles, "customers can't just walk. There's nowhere else to go." Airlines that switch give up their delivery slot and wait up to a decade.

Kyler's conclusion: Boeing's competitive position is secure *despite* its execution problems. The balance sheet was stabilized, the new CEO (Kelly Ortberg) was fixing quality, and the $636 billion backlog proved demand wasn't the issue -- supply was.

### Hormel vs. Food Industry Peers

Kyler compared Hormel against other packaged food companies on:

- **Brand strength:** Hormel owns 40+ recognizable brands (SPAM, Skippy, Planters, Jennie-O)
- **Margin resilience:** During past commodity spikes (2008-2009, COVID), Hormel eventually recovered margins
- **Catalyst exposure:** Hormel is heavily weighted toward protein, which benefits from the GLP-1 trend
- **Balance sheet:** 9.2x interest coverage ratio, net debt to EBITDA of 2.2x, no near-term debt maturities

The result: Hormel stood out as a high-quality name trading at 2009-level prices, with brand strength providing a margin of safety that weaker food companies lacked.

### Alibaba vs. Chinese E-Commerce Peers

When comparing Alibaba to JD.com and Pinduoduo, Kyler looked at:

- **Market position:** Alibaba controls 40-45% of Chinese e-commerce (still #1)
- **Cloud business:** Larger than the 2nd, 3rd, and 4th players *combined*, with AI cloud revenue growing triple digits for 9 consecutive quarters
- **Balance sheet:** $41 billion in net cash
- **Catalysts:** Policy shift from punishment to partnership, demographic tailwinds, instant commerce growth

Despite fierce competition from PDD and Meituan, Alibaba's diversified business (e-commerce + cloud + logistics) and massive cash position made it stand out.

## What This Step Produces

By the end of this comparison process, Kyler has:

- **Identified the highest-quality company** in each industry group (when possible)
- **Pruned clearly weaker companies** from the list
- **Kept backup options** that offer higher returns despite lower quality
- **Developed a deep understanding** of the industry landscape

The surviving companies earn what Kyler calls "the right to deeper work" -- which is the qualitative deep dive covered in [04-QUALITATIVE-DEEP-DIVE.md](04-QUALITATIVE-DEEP-DIVE.md).

> **Key Idea:** "While this step may seem straightforward on the surface, it takes a lot of time to do well. I'm still improving here, but I've found this process extremely helpful for understanding an industry and filtering up to higher-quality ideas."

## Quick Summary

| What Kyler Does | What He's Looking For |
|---|---|
| Reviews business cycle history | Has the company survived downturns before? Is this one comparable? |
| Checks margin trends | Any steady decline = permanent pass list |
| Assesses industry pressures | Temporary headwinds are OK; permanent headwinds are not |
| Compares competitors side by side | Leverage, returns, margins, niches, growth, dilution, potential returns |
| Defines quality | Balance sheet strength first, then superior niches/margins |
| Decides who stays | Weaker companies stay only if: no missed failures, higher returns, limited alternatives |
