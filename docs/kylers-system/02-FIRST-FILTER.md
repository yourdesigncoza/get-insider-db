# Step 2: The First Filter

*Once a company makes it onto Kyler's list, he runs it through five basic checks. This is the gate that separates "interesting idea" from "worth spending real time on." Most stocks fail here -- and that's the point.*

Think of this like a bouncer at a club. You don't need to know everything about someone to decide if they get in. You just need to catch the obvious problems. These five checks are fast, fundamental, and brutal. A company can look exciting on the surface but fail here because the numbers don't support the story.

---

## The 5 Basic Checks

Every stock must pass all five. If it fails on any one, it either gets removed from the list or flagged for a very specific deeper look.

---

### 1. Solvency: Can This Company Survive?

**What it means:** **Solvency** (see [09-GLOSSARY.md](09-GLOSSARY.md)) is whether a company can pay its bills -- not just today, but over the next few years. A company that runs out of cash goes bankrupt, and shareholders usually lose everything.

**The Analogy:** Imagine you're evaluating a friend who wants to borrow money to start a business. Before you even look at the business plan, you'd ask: "How much cash do you have? How much do you owe? Can you make your loan payments?" That's the solvency check.

**What Kyler Looks At:**

- **Total cash**: How much money does the company have on hand?
- **Current debt**: What bills are coming due in the next 12 months?
- **Long-term debt**: What's the total debt burden over the life of all loans?
- **Free Cash Flow (FCF)** (see [09-GLOSSARY.md](09-GLOSSARY.md)): Is the company generating cash from its operations, or burning through it?

**What "good" looks like varies by industry.** Industries with large physical assets (factories, warehouses, pipelines) can carry higher **leverage** (debt relative to their size) and still be manageable. A software company with the same debt level would be more alarming because it doesn't have hard assets to fall back on.

> **Key Idea:** Being leveraged alone doesn't disqualify a company, but it does trigger a deeper look.

**The Deeper Look (When Triggered):**

If the balance sheet looks worrying, Kyler digs into:

- **Debt maturity schedule**: When exactly does each chunk of debt come due? A company with $500M in debt due in 5 years is in a very different position than one with $500M due next quarter.
- **Available credit facilities**: Can they borrow more if needed?
- **Potential asset sales**: Do they own anything they could sell to raise cash?
- **Other cash sources**: Insurance payouts, tax refunds, licensing deals?

**The Critical Question:** If Kyler is unsure whether the company will survive, the next question is: *Has the stock already fallen enough to reflect that risk?*

- If yes (the risk is "priced in"), he keeps digging to reach a conclusion on survivability.
- If no (the market still seems optimistic despite real solvency risk), the company is removed from the list.

**Real Example -- Cooper Standard ($CPS):**

In 2021, Cooper Standard burned through $211 million in FCF. They had just $248 million in cash against $1 billion in debt, with $630 million due within three years. Many investors assumed bankruptcy was coming.

But the stock had already fallen 97% from highs. The solvency risk was priced in. Kyler dug deeper and found that management had refinanced debt (pushing payments out 3 years and reducing the near-term total by 30%), cut $241 million in sustainable costs, and had $266 million in available liquidity. The company would survive -- and the stock was priced as if it wouldn't.

---

### 2. Dilution: Is Management Enriching Itself at Your Expense?

**What it means:** **Dilution** (see [09-GLOSSARY.md](09-GLOSSARY.md)) happens when a company issues new shares of stock. Each new share is like cutting a pizza into more slices -- your slice gets smaller even though the pizza hasn't gotten bigger.

**The Analogy:** Imagine you and three friends own a lemonade stand equally (25% each). One day, the manager gives himself a 25% stake without putting up any money. Now everyone else owns 20%. The stand didn't get more valuable -- your share just shrank. That's dilution.

**Kyler distinguishes between two types of dilution:**

| Type | What It Looks Like | How Kyler Feels |
|---|---|---|
| **Meaningless dilution** | Shares issued to pay management lavishly, cover **stock-based compensation (SBC)** (see [09-GLOSSARY.md](09-GLOSSARY.md)) without corresponding growth | Red flag |
| **Value-creating dilution** | Shares issued to fund acquisitions that grow revenue and profit faster than the dilution | Acceptable if per-share value grows |

**What Kyler Looks At:**

- **Per-share growth**: Even if total revenue is growing, is revenue *per share* growing? If a company doubles its revenue but triples its share count, shareholders are worse off.
- **SBC at 5%+ without growth**: This is a red flag. Management is taking from shareholders without delivering results.
- **Issuing shares to pay debt**: This is near-immediate disqualification. It means the company can't generate enough cash to meet its obligations and is funding survival by stealing from shareholders.

> **Key Idea:** "By 'meaningless,' I mean whether capital is being used to line management's pockets at the expense of shareholders. Or is it being used for acquisitions that have historically created value?"

---

### 3. Revenue Growth: Will the Business Get Bigger?

**What it means:** Is the company bringing in more money over time? Revenue is the total amount of money a company earns from selling its products or services -- before any expenses are paid.

**The Analogy:** If you run a lemonade stand and sold 100 cups last summer but 120 this summer (at the same price), your revenue grew 20%. Revenue growth tells you whether demand for what the company sells is increasing.

**What Kyler Looks At:**

- **Historical growth**: Has revenue generally trended up over the past 5-10 years?
- **Known catalysts**: Are there specific reasons to expect growth? (Industry recovery, new products, regulatory changes, demographic shifts)
- **Earnings calls**: If Kyler is unsure, he digs into what management is saying about their growth plans and whether he believes them.

This is usually a simpler check than the others. The question is straightforward: based on what I know about this company and its industry, will revenue grow? If the answer is clearly no and there are no catalysts to change that, the company comes off the list.

---

### 4. ROCE/ROIC: Does This Business Use Money Well?

**What it means:** **ROCE** (Return on Capital Employed) and **ROIC** (Return on Invested Capital) measure how efficiently a company turns invested money into profit. A company with 15% ROIC generates $15 of profit for every $100 of capital invested in the business. (See [09-GLOSSARY.md](09-GLOSSARY.md) for detailed definitions.)

**The Analogy:** Imagine two kids each get $100 to start a business. Kid A turns it into $115 by the end of the year. Kid B turns it into $104. Kid A is better at using capital. Over many years, that difference compounds into a massive gap. You want to invest in Kid A's business.

**What Kyler Looks At:**

- **Historical median ROCE/ROIC**: He wants to see a track record, not just one good year.
- **General minimum: ~6%**. Below that, the company is barely earning more than it costs to stay in business.
- **Ideal: ~10% median or higher**. This signals a business that consistently earns well above its cost of capital.

**The Cyclical Exception:**

Some industries go through big ups and downs (called **cyclical** businesses -- see [09-GLOSSARY.md](09-GLOSSARY.md)). During downturns, their ROIC can look terrible. What matters is the full-cycle picture.

> **Real Example:** Cooper Standard ($CPS) has a median ROIC/ROCE of about 8.5% over 20 years -- below the ideal 10%. But during up-cycles, it hits the mid-teens or higher. Kyler doesn't disqualify it because the cyclical pattern is clear and the up-cycle returns are strong.

Kyler has become more lenient with this filter over time. He mainly wants to avoid businesses with chronically low returns -- the ones that never seem to earn decent money no matter what the economy does.

---

### 5. Valuation: Is It Actually Cheap?

**What it means:** **Valuation** (see [09-GLOSSARY.md](09-GLOSSARY.md)) is figuring out what price is fair for a stock. Just because a stock has fallen a lot doesn't make it cheap. And just because a stock has a high price tag doesn't make it expensive. It's all relative to what the business actually earns.

**The Analogy:** A $500,000 house in Manhattan could be a steal. A $500,000 house in a rural town with no jobs might be way overpriced. The "cheapness" of a price depends entirely on what you're getting for it.

**What Kyler Compares:**

He looks at several **valuation metrics** (ratios that compare a stock's price to what the business earns) against their *own historical averages*:

| Metric | What It Measures |
|---|---|
| **P/S** (Price-to-Sales) | Stock price relative to revenue per share |
| **P/FCF** (Price-to-Free-Cash-Flow) | Stock price relative to cash generated per share |
| **EV/FCF** (Enterprise-Value-to-FCF) | Total company value (including debt) relative to cash generated |
| **EV/EBITDA** | Total company value relative to operating earnings before depreciation |

See [09-GLOSSARY.md](09-GLOSSARY.md) for full definitions of each.

**The Normalization Trick:**

If a company's margins are currently below their historical average (common in turnarounds), comparing today's P/FCF to history is misleading. The current FCF is depressed, making the ratio look expensive.

Kyler fixes this by **normalizing**: he estimates what FCF *would be* if margins returned to historical levels, then calculates a "normalized P/FCF." This gives a much more accurate picture of whether the stock is truly cheap.

**The Hurdle Rate:**

Every stock must be able to reasonably meet Kyler's **hurdle rate** (see [09-GLOSSARY.md](09-GLOSSARY.md)) of roughly **30% per year**. By this point, he's spent enough time to define a reasonable time frame for each company.

He estimates potential **CAGR** (compound annual growth rate -- see [09-GLOSSARY.md](09-GLOSSARY.md)) by applying normalized margins to a reasonable future revenue level. This is a rough exercise, but it prevents him from spending days on a company that has little chance of meeting his return target.

> **Key Idea:** "Cheap" is relative. A stock at 25x FCF might be cheap if it's growing 30% per year. A stock at 10x FCF might be overvalued if revenue is shrinking and margins are eroding. Context is everything.

## The Walmart Example: Great Business, Wrong Price

Kyler uses Walmart ($WMT) to show that passing four checks doesn't matter if you fail the fifth:

| Check | Result |
|---|---|
| Revenue growth | 4.33% annually over 20 years |
| FCF growth | 6.6% annually over 20 years |
| ROIC | 15.39% average over 20 years |
| Solvency | Solid |
| Dilution | Shares *decreasing* by 2.25%/year (buybacks) |
| **Valuation** | **Trades at 56x FCF** (FCF yield of ~1.78%) |

Walmart is a phenomenal business. It passes every quality check with flying colors. But at 56x FCF, Kyler's money would earn a tiny return. He can't meet his 30% hurdle rate from that price.

**Result:** Walmart goes on the watchlist with a target price of around $34, or 20x P/FCF. That's still probably too high, but it would be worth another look at that level.

> **Key Idea:** The price at which Kyler sets his watchlist target is often dramatically below where the stock trades. Patience means waiting for your price, even if it takes years -- or never comes.

## What This Step Also Includes

The First Filter isn't purely about running five numbers through a checklist. As Kyler reviews each company, he also picks up on:

- **Business cycle history**: How has this company performed during previous downturns and recoveries?
- **Margin trends**: Are margins generally stable, improving, or steadily eroding?
- **Industry pressures**: Are there **headwinds** (negative forces) or **tailwinds** (positive forces) he didn't know about? (See [09-GLOSSARY.md](09-GLOSSARY.md))
- **Basic competitor comparison**: Without going deep yet, how does this company roughly compare to others in the same industry?

These observations inform the next steps of the process, even if they don't formally "pass" or "fail" a company at this stage.

---

## Summary: The First Filter at a Glance

| # | Check | Key Question | Instant Fail If... |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Solvency** | Can this company survive? | Risk isn't priced in and survival is uncertain |
| 2 | **Dilution** | Is management stealing from shareholders? | Shares issued to pay debt, or 5%+ SBC with no growth |
| 3 | **Revenue Growth** | Will the business get bigger? | No growth and no catalysts to change that |
| 4 | **ROCE/ROIC** | Does the business use money well? | Chronically below ~6%, worse than all competitors |
| 5 | **Valuation** | Is it actually cheap? | Can't reasonably meet 30% annual hurdle rate |

Companies that pass all five checks earn the right to deeper analysis. Those that fail get removed or go on a watchlist at a lower price.
