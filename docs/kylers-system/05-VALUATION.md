# What Is This Company Actually Worth?

*Step 5 of Kyler's system. You've done the research. Now it's time to put a price tag on the business and decide if the math actually works.*

---

## Kyler's Valuation Philosophy

Before getting into the numbers, it's important to understand *how Kyler thinks* about valuation. His philosophy is surprisingly simple.

> "I don't use complex models, and I don't believe you need them. An investable idea should be obvious."

Think of it like buying a house. You don't need a 50-page spreadsheet to know if a house is a good deal. If a solid house in a good neighborhood is selling for half the price of similar houses, it's probably worth looking at. If you need a calculator and three hours to figure out whether it's cheap, it's probably not cheap enough.

> "And because a model will always be just that -- a model. It will almost certainly be wrong, so I keep things simple and aim for a general, reasonable valuation over a sensible time frame."

Kyler's valuation isn't about precision. It's about direction. He wants to confirm that the upside is real and large enough to be worth the risk.

---

## The Four Things Kyler Models

Kyler only estimates four things. That's it. No discounted cash flow models with 47 assumptions. No Monte Carlo simulations. Four inputs:

### 1. Revenue

**What it is:** The total money the company brings in from selling its products or services (see [Glossary](09-GLOSSARY.md)). If a lemonade stand sells 100 cups at $1 each, revenue is $100.

**How Kyler estimates it:** He builds revenue from the bottom up using:

- **Industry baseline growth:** How fast is the overall industry growing? If the food industry grows 2-3% per year, that's the starting point.
- **Company initiatives:** What is the company doing to grow faster than the industry? New products, new markets, cost-cutting that enables reinvestment?
- **Long-term targets:** What has management committed to publicly?
- **Catalysts:** What additional growth drivers exist? (Identified in [04-QUALITATIVE-DEEP-DIVE.md](04-QUALITATIVE-DEEP-DIVE.md))

For some companies, Kyler breaks revenue down by segment when the business has distinct parts growing at different speeds.

### 2. FCF Margins

**What it is:** **Free Cash Flow (FCF)** is the actual cash left over after a company pays all its bills and invests in maintaining its business (see [Glossary](09-GLOSSARY.md)). The **FCF Margin** is what percentage of revenue becomes free cash flow.

If a company earns $100 in revenue and has $10 in free cash flow, its FCF margin is 10%.

**How Kyler estimates it:** He looks at where margins have been historically and what's changing:

> "If a company has cut significant costs and isn't adding them back as revenue grows, I'm comfortable modeling margins above prior cycle levels."

He also considers:
- Are cost-cutting programs already in place and delivering results?
- Is the company benefiting from operating leverage (fixed costs being spread over more revenue)?
- Are there any headwinds that will keep margins below historical levels?

In earlier versions of his framework, Kyler modeled this as Operating Cash Flow Margin minus Capex separately. Today he simplifies to FCF margins directly.

### 3. FCF Multiple

**What it is:** A **multiple** is a number you multiply the company's earnings or cash flow by to estimate its total value (see [Glossary](09-GLOSSARY.md)). If a company generates $10 in FCF and trades at a 20x multiple, the market is saying the company is worth $200.

Think of it like this: if a rental property generates $10,000 per year in profit and similar properties sell for 15 times their annual profit, the property is worth about $150,000. The "15x" is the multiple.

**Why it matters so much:** The multiple is by far the biggest swing factor in any valuation. A company generating $100 million in FCF is worth $1.5 billion at 15x but $2.5 billion at 25x -- a massive difference from changing just one number.

**How Kyler estimates it:**

> "Deciding on a multiple is by far the most subjective and difficult part of the process. Short-term multiples are often driven by sentiment, making them difficult to predict years in advance."

Kyler starts with an industry baseline and adjusts for the individual company:

| Factor | How It Affects the Multiple |
|---|---|
| **Business Quality** (ROIC/ROCE + competitive advantages) | Higher quality = higher multiple |
| **Historical Multiples** (useful context, but the business may be different today) | Anchoring point, not a guarantee |
| **Growth** | Faster growth = higher multiple |
| **Leverage** (debt relative to peers or history) | More debt = lower multiple |
| **Risks** | More risk = lower multiple |

**Important industry rules of thumb:**

> "Cyclical businesses will almost always carry lower baseline multiples due to the risk of downturns, regardless of quality. Healthcare businesses typically warrant higher baseline multiples due to regulation, development timelines, and the cost required to bring products to market."

**How to think about it step by step:**

1. Start with what's normal for the industry (e.g., cyclical industrials might average 12-15x FCF; consumer staples might average 25-28x)
2. Adjust up for above-average growth, strong competitive advantages, or improving fundamentals
3. Adjust down for above-average leverage, elevated risks, or deteriorating fundamentals
4. The goal: "balance realism with quality and direction"

### 4. Change in Shares Outstanding

**What it is:** The total number of ownership slices (shares) of a company. If a company is a pizza with 10 slices and you own 1, you own 10%. If the company creates 10 new slices (now 20 total), your 1 slice is now only 5%. That's **dilution** -- your ownership got watered down (see [Glossary](09-GLOSSARY.md)).

**How Kyler estimates it:**

> "If there is no clear direction, I leave share count flat."

He looks at the recent trend:
- **Share count going down** (buybacks): The company is buying back its own stock, concentrating ownership. Good for shareholders.
- **Share count going up** (dilution): The company is issuing new shares, watering down ownership. Bad for shareholders unless the money is being used productively.
- **Flat:** No clear trend. Kyler leaves it unchanged.

## The 30% Hurdle Rate

After building his valuation, Kyler applies a strict test:

> "In the end, I need to believe that the position can generate **30%+ annual returns**. If it doesn't meet that requirement, I won't invest."

A **hurdle rate** is the minimum return an investor requires before committing money (see [Glossary](09-GLOSSARY.md)). Kyler's is 30% per year -- meaning if he invests today, the stock needs to roughly double every 2.5 years.

That's a high bar. It means Kyler passes on a lot of opportunities that most investors would consider attractive. A stock with 15% annual upside? Not enough. 20%? Still not enough. It needs to clear 30%.

**Why so high?** Because Kyler invests in turnarounds. Turnarounds are inherently riskier than buying stable, growing businesses. The high hurdle rate compensates for that risk. If things go wrong (and with turnarounds, things often go wrong), the large potential upside provides a cushion.

### The One Exception

> "There is one major exception to my hurdle rate. If I find a company with a long runway of six years or more that can compound at 20%+ annually and is led by a top-tier CEO, I may still take a position despite missing my hurdle rate."

Kyler explains the logic:

> "There is value both in the likelihood that an exceptional CEO beats even optimistic forecasts and in the duration over which strong returns compound."

Think of it like compound interest. A 20% return over 6 years turns $100 into $299. A 30% return over 3 years turns $100 into $220. The longer compounding period with a great CEO can sometimes be worth more.

These positions are "usually smaller, but still worth holding."

## Walking Through Real Examples

Let's see exactly how Kyler puts these four inputs together. Follow along step by step.

### Example 1: Cooper Standard (CPS) -- The Original

This was one of Kyler's earliest published valuations. The stock was at $22 when he first wrote about it.

**Step 1: Revenue**

Kyler's thesis: Auto sales will recover to 2018 levels (~18 million units). CPS's revenue is directly tied to the number of cars built.

- Revenue at recovery: **$3.7 billion** (up ~32% from the trailing twelve months of $2.82 billion)
- Time frame: By 2028-2029

**Step 2: Margins**

Kyler looked at CPS's historical margins:
- Peak FCF margin in 2016: about 5.8%
- He estimated margins would slightly *exceed* the prior peak because of aggressive cost savings ($56M in 2023 + $185M in 2021-2022)

Doing the math:
- Operating Cash Flow (OCF) Margin: **9.25%**
- Capex: **3% of revenue**
- Implied FCF Margin: approximately **6.25%**

Free Cash Flow calculation:
- $3.7 billion revenue x 6.25% FCF margin = **$231 million in FCF**

**Step 3: Multiple**

CPS is a cyclical industrial company (lower baseline multiple). At its best, CPS previously traded at 12x FCF.

- Multiple: **12x FCF**

**Step 4: Shares Outstanding**

Kyler didn't model significant changes in share count.

**The Math:**

| Input | Value |
|---|---|
| 2028 Revenue | $3.7 billion |
| FCF Margin | ~6.25% |
| FCF | $231 million |
| Multiple | 12x |
| **Market Cap** | **$2.77 billion** |
| **Share Price** | **~$158** |

From a starting price of $22, that implied **600%+ upside, or a 72% CAGR** over roughly 4 years. Well above the 30% hurdle.

### Example 2: Hormel (HRL) -- The Boring Compounder

Hormel was trading around $24.20 when Kyler took a position. Here's how he built the valuation.

**Step 1: Revenue**

Hormel is not a high-growth company. Kyler assumed modest growth driven by pricing, brand focus, and gradual volume normalization.

Starting point: ~$12.0 billion (adjusted for a $100M divestiture)

- 2026: 2% growth
- 2027: 3% growth
- 2028: 3% growth

| Year | Revenue |
|---|---|
| 2025 (base) | $12.0B |
| 2026 | $12.24B |
| 2027 | $12.61B |
| **2028** | **$12.98B** |

**Step 2: Margins**

Kyler modeled 400 basis points of margin improvement from 2025 levels (a **basis point** is 1/100th of a percent, so 400 basis points = 4 percentage points; see [Glossary](09-GLOSSARY.md)). This was driven by:

- Price hikes flowing through (remember the lag from [03-DEEP-ANALYSIS.md](03-DEEP-ANALYSIS.md))
- Transform & Modernize cost savings continuing
- Working capital normalization
- Capex moderating

Result:
- 2028 FCF Margin: **8.4%**
- 2028 FCF: $12.98B x 8.4% = **$1.09 billion**

**Step 3: Multiple**

Hormel is a high-quality consumer staples company with durable brands and a 60-year dividend growth record. Over the last 10 years:
- Median P/FCF: 26.6x
- Median EV/FCF: 28x

Kyler applied **28x EV/FCF**. His reasoning:
- Durable brands justify a premium multiple
- ~4.8% dividend yield attracts income-focused capital as interest rates fall
- He held total debt constant at $2.86 billion

**Step 4: Shares Outstanding**

No change assumed.

**The Math:**

| Input | Value |
|---|---|
| 2028 Revenue | $12.98B |
| FCF Margin | 8.4% |
| FCF | $1.09B |
| Multiple (EV/FCF) | 28x |
| Enterprise Value | $30.52B |
| Minus Debt | -$2.86B |
| **Market Cap** | **$27.66B** |
| Shares Outstanding | ~549.6M |
| **Share Price** | **$50.29** |

Plus dividends of ~$3.56/share over the holding period:

| Result | Value |
|---|---|
| Total value per share | ~$53.85 |
| Starting price | $24.20 |
| **Total upside** | **~122%** |
| **CAGR** | **~31%** |

As a sanity check, Kyler noted this implies a P/FCF of 25.3x -- well below the 10-year median. That gave him confidence the multiple wasn't aggressive.

### Example 3: Alibaba (BABA) -- The International Giant

Alibaba was at ~$157 when Kyler updated his thesis. This is a more complex business, but the valuation approach stays the same.

**Step 1: Revenue**

Kyler modeled by segment because Alibaba's parts grow at very different rates:
- E-commerce: high single-digit growth
- International commerce: low double-digit growth
- Cloud: low 20% growth
- Others: mid single-digit growth

After applying a 7.5% reduction for intersegment eliminations (internal transactions between divisions):

- **FY2029 Revenue: 1.53 trillion yuan** (~$214 billion USD at 0.14 exchange rate)

**Step 2: Margins**

Kyler modeled a **20% FCF margin** -- below 2023 peaks and well below pre-COVID levels. This was conservative, backed by:
- Cloud becoming a bigger (higher-margin) part of the business
- Rising ad monetization from instant commerce
- Cost discipline and operational focus

- FY2029 FCF: 1.53T yuan x 20% = **306.6 billion yuan** (~$42.9 billion USD)

**Step 3: Multiple**

This is where geography matters. Kyler explicitly discounted the multiple because of China:

> "If this was a US-based company I would likely say into the low 20s multiple, but given China continues to carry large amounts of pessimism, I am building in it never fully goes away."

- Multiple: **18x FCF**

**Step 4: Shares Outstanding**

Alibaba was actively buying back shares ($19.1 billion remaining in its program). Kyler assumed ~12% shrinkage in ADR count by 2029.

- ADRs outstanding: 2.39 billion --> **2.12 billion**

**The Math:**

| Input | Value |
|---|---|
| FY2029 Revenue | ~$214B USD |
| FCF Margin | 20% |
| FCF | ~$42.9B |
| FCF per ADR | $42.9B / 2.12B = **$20.24** |
| Multiple | 18x |
| **Share Price** | $20.24 x 18 = **$364** |
| Plus dividends ($1.05/yr x 4 years) | +$4.20 |
| **Total Value** | **~$368** |

| Result | Value |
|---|---|
| Starting price | $157 |
| Target price | $368 |
| **Total upside** | **~134%** |
| **CAGR** | **~23%** |

Note: At 23% CAGR, this falls *below* Kyler's 30% hurdle rate. He already owned the position from a lower cost basis and noted he would need the stock to fall below $125 before adding more.

### Example 4: Ryman Healthcare (RYM.NZ) -- When Kyler Passes

Not every valuation leads to an investment. Ryman Healthcare is a good example of a company Kyler found interesting but ultimately passed on.

**The numbers:**
- 2029 Revenue: NZ$1.06 billion (7% annual growth)
- Operating Cash Flow Margin: 75% (Ryman's model takes in years of cash upfront from residents)
- OCF: NZ$795 million
- EV/OCF Multiple: 11x (discounted from historical 12-16x due to past mistakes and slower growth)
- Debt: ~NZ$2 billion
- Market Cap: ~NZ$6.75 billion
- Share Price: NZ$6.65

From NZ$2.94, that implies **126% upside or a ~23% CAGR**.

Kyler's conclusion:

> "In the end, it does not clear my hurdle rate but is still a very interesting company and industry to keep an eye on in NZ."

This is discipline in action. The company was interesting, the setup was attractive, but the math didn't clear 30%. So Kyler shared the idea with readers but didn't invest his own money.

## The Reasonable Worst Case

Kyler doesn't just model the upside. For every investment, he also estimates a **reasonable worst case** -- what happens if things go wrong but not catastrophically.

**Hormel worst case:**
> "The downside case for Hormel is not a broken business, but a slow stagnation... Free cash flow stays positive and slightly above 2025 levels but well below historical norms... I believe around $20/share represents a realistic lower bound outside of a recession."

From a starting price of $24.20, that's about 17% downside in the worst case -- against 122% upside in the base case. That asymmetry (limited downside, large upside) is exactly what Kyler looks for.

**Ryman worst case:**
Kyler used tangible book value as the floor. Ryman owns NZ$4.17 billion in physical assets (retirement villages). Even at a low 0.6x price-to-tangible-book-value, the stock would be worth NZ$2.54 -- only about 14% below the current price.

> **Key Idea:** The worst case isn't about getting the exact number right. It's about asking: "If I'm wrong, how bad could it get?" If the answer is "I lose a little," that's very different from "I lose everything."

## Quick Summary: The Valuation Process

| Step | What Kyler Does |
|---|---|
| **Revenue** | Industry baseline growth + company initiatives + targets + catalysts |
| **FCF Margins** | Historical margins adjusted for cost cuts, pricing, and any structural changes |
| **FCF Multiple** | Industry baseline, adjusted for quality, growth, leverage, historical norms, and risks |
| **Share Count** | Flat unless there's a clear buyback or dilution trend |
| **Hurdle Rate** | Must return 30%+ per year (exception: 20%+ over 6+ years with top-tier CEO) |
| **Worst Case** | What happens if things go wrong? Is downside contained? |

---

## The Gut Check

After building his valuation, Kyler always does a sanity check. Does the implied valuation make sense compared to the company's own history and peers?

For Hormel: "As a gut check, this comes out to P/FCF of 25.3x, well below the 10-year median." That means his base case assumes the stock trades *cheaper* than it has historically -- a conservative assumption.

For Boeing: He compared both price-to-sales (2.25x, implying a $265B market cap) and price-to-FCF (26x) to make sure they told a consistent story.

If the gut check fails -- if the numbers require assumptions that feel unreasonable -- Kyler goes back and adjusts or walks away.

## The Bottom Line

Kyler's valuation approach is intentionally simple. Four inputs. A reasonable time frame. A high bar to clear. And always a worst-case estimate to make sure the downside is survivable.

The power isn't in the model's complexity. It's in the hours of research that went into every assumption -- the business cycle analysis ([03-DEEP-ANALYSIS.md](03-DEEP-ANALYSIS.md)), the qualitative work on moats and management ([04-QUALITATIVE-DEEP-DIVE.md](04-QUALITATIVE-DEEP-DIVE.md)), and the fundamental filters that brought the company to this point ([02-FIRST-FILTER.md](02-FIRST-FILTER.md)).

> "An investable idea should be obvious."

If the valuation requires heroic assumptions to work, the idea isn't obvious enough.
