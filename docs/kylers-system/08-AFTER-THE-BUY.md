# 08 - After the Buy: Owning Stocks, Psychology, and Selling

*Kyler's framework for monitoring positions, staying rational through volatility, and knowing when to walk away.*

---

You have done the research. You scored the stock, sized the position, and clicked "buy." Many investors think the hard part is over.

It is not. It is just beginning.

The period between buying and selling is where most investors lose their edge. Not because their analysis was wrong, but because their emotions take over. They panic during drops. They get greedy during rallies. They chase the next shiny thing.

This chapter covers how Kyler stays disciplined after the purchase -- what he tracks, how he handles fear, when he sells, and how he learns from every closed position.

---

## The Analogy: Planting a Tree

Buying a stock is like planting a fruit tree. You spent weeks choosing the right tree (research), the right spot in your yard (position sizing), and prepared the soil (timing). But after planting, the work continues.

You need to water it. Check for disease. Protect it from storms. And most importantly, you need to resist the urge to dig it up every time you do not see fruit yet.

A tree does not grow fruit the week you plant it. Neither does a stock. Kyler typically holds positions for **1-4 years** to let his thesis play out. During that time, patience is not passive -- it is active, informed, and deliberate.

---

## Conviction = Information Buffer

This is one of Kyler's most important ideas, and he returns to it repeatedly:

> *"Conviction comes from understanding how the real business is evolving over time."*

Most people think conviction is a feeling -- "I just believe in this stock." For Kyler, conviction is built from facts. The more you know about a company, the harder it is for headlines to scare you into selling.

> *"That's what conviction really is: a buffer between you and the noise."*

Think of conviction like armor. Every earnings call you listen to, every report you read, every competitor you study -- each one adds another layer of protection between you and the daily fear factory of financial news.

Without that armor, you are exposed. Every headline about tariffs, recessions, or interest rates hits you directly. With it, you can evaluate whether the news actually changes anything for your specific company.

---

## What Kyler Tracks After Buying

Kyler does not just buy and forget. He stays close to every company he owns, consistently reviewing earnings, watching catalysts, tracking management, and checking the risks he flagged during his original research.

He tracks five key questions:

### 1. Are the catalysts I expected actually showing up?

When Kyler bought the stock, he identified specific events that should improve the business -- margin expansion, a new product launch, regulatory clearance, rising industry demand. He tracks whether these are happening on schedule.

If a catalyst that was supposed to arrive in Q2 gets pushed to Q4, that is worth noting but not necessarily alarming. If it gets cancelled entirely, that changes the thesis.

### 2. Is management saying one thing and doing another?

This is a red flag detector. If the CEO promised to cut costs and instead expanded the workforce, something is off. If management said they would not dilute shareholders and then issued new stock to fund acquisitions, the original thesis may need updating.

### 3. Are margins shifting in ways I didn't model?

**Margins** (see [Glossary](09-GLOSSARY.md)) are the percentage of revenue a company keeps as profit. If Kyler modeled 12% margins and they are coming in at 8%, he needs to understand why. Is it temporary (a one-time cost) or structural (a permanent change in the business)?

### 4. Are competitors pulling ahead?

Even a great company can lose if a competitor leapfrogs them. Kyler watches the competitive landscape, not just his own holdings.

### 5. Which macro or geopolitical events actually move the needle?

Not all news matters. Kyler looks for the small number of external events that genuinely affect the businesses he owns, and ignores the rest.

## Psychology: Beating Fear with Information

This is the section that separates Kyler's approach from most investing advice. He does not preach meditation, cold showers, or positive thinking. His method is bluntly practical:

> *"I don't try to conquer emotions with mindset hacks or willpower. I try to counter them with information."*

The logic is simple: **fear thrives in the unknown.** When you do not understand what is happening to your investments, every piece of bad news feels catastrophic. When you DO understand, you can tell the difference between real threats and noise.

> *"If I understand what is really happening, I'm less likely to panic. I can step back, zoom out, and evaluate my positions based on facts rather than noise."*

### Case Study: The April 2025 Tariff Scare

This is the best example of Kyler's psychology in action, and it is worth telling in detail.

In April 2025, the U.S. government announced a new round of tariffs on imports. Financial media erupted. Headlines screamed about trade wars, recession risks, and market crashes. Stocks dropped sharply across the board, including many of Kyler's holdings.

Here is what most investors did: they panicked. They saw their portfolios dropping day after day. They saw scary headlines on every news site. And they sold -- locking in losses to "protect" themselves from more pain.

Here is what Kyler did: he went back to his research.

He already knew the companies he owned. He had studied their supply chains, their geographic exposure, their pricing power, their balance sheets. He asked himself: "Will these tariffs actually destroy these businesses?"

The answer, for his holdings, was no. They would take a hit, but not a fatal one. The core theses were intact. The catalysts were still in place. The valuations were now even cheaper.

So instead of selling, **he bought more.**

Kyler writes: *"The headlines made it sound like the sky was falling. But because I had done the work, and continued doing it, I saw the situation more clearly. I knew the companies I owned would take a hit, but not a fatal one. I added instead of selling. That decision resulted in materially better returns in 2025."*

And then the crucial reflection:

> *"If I had let that fear take hold without re-grounding in the fundamentals, I might have sold something great or chased a shiny new stock."*

This is conviction as armor in action. The research he had already done protected him from making a terrible decision.

> **Key Idea:** The tariff scare story is not about being brave or contrarian. It is about having done enough homework that the fear could not override the facts. Kyler did not "believe" his stocks would be fine. He *knew* the specific reasons why -- because he had studied the actual businesses, not just the stock prices.

## When Kyler Sells

Selling is one of the hardest parts of investing. Hold too long and you watch gains evaporate. Sell too early and you leave money on the table.

Beyond the opportunity replacement scenarios covered in [06-THE-DECISION.md](06-THE-DECISION.md) (where a clearly better stock replaces an existing one), Kyler sells in three specific situations:

### 1. Target Price Reached, Forward Returns Don't Meet Hurdle Rate

The stock has hit or exceeded Kyler's price target. At the new higher price, the expected annual return going forward falls below his 30% hurdle rate.

This is the happy ending. The thesis played out. The stock went where Kyler thought it would go. But now the math says the best returns are behind it, so it is time to move the capital somewhere with more upside.

### 2. Stock Moves Up Rapidly, Forward Returns Fall Below 10-15%/Year

Sometimes a stock jumps much faster than expected. Maybe good earnings surprised the market, or sentiment shifted overnight.

Kyler writes: *"When a stock moves sharply, it often spends long periods consolidating. In those cases, locking in gains and redeploying capital into an opportunity that is earlier in its cycle is usually preferable."*

**Consolidating** (see [Glossary](09-GLOSSARY.md)) means a stock stops going up and trades sideways for a while after a big move. If forward returns drop below 10-15% per year, the stock is likely to tread water. Kyler would rather move that capital into a stock that still has its big move ahead of it.

### 3. Fundamental Change Breaks the Thesis

This is the bad outcome. Something changed that invalidates the reason you bought the stock in the first place.

Maybe the company lost a major contract. Maybe the CEO who was driving the turnaround resigned. Maybe a new regulation made the business model unworkable. Maybe you discovered something in the financials you missed.

Kyler writes: *"If something does change, and the facts break the thesis, I'll walk away. But I only know that because I'm paying attention."*

The key word is **"facts."** Not feelings, not headlines, not what other people are doing. A 30% drop in the stock price is NOT a thesis break. A 30% drop in revenue because the company's biggest customer left IS a thesis break.

> **Key Idea:** Notice what is NOT on this list: "the stock went down." Kyler does not sell because the price dropped. He sells because the *business* changed. This is the fundamental difference between investing and trading. The stock and the business are not the same thing.

## Post-Mortem Reviews: Learning from Every Position

After closing a position -- whether it was a winner or a loser -- Kyler conducts a review. This is the feedback loop that makes the entire system better over time.

> *"I revisit my original thesis and compare it to what actually happened, looking for mistakes, blind spots, or faulty assumptions."*

He asks questions like:
- Was my worst-case scenario accurate, or did I underestimate the risk?
- Did I correctly identify the catalysts, or did different ones drive the outcome?
- Was my valuation model reasonable, or was I too optimistic on margins or growth?
- Did I size the position correctly given the outcome?
- What would I do differently next time?

> *"Over time, this feedback loop helps refine my process and, ideally, eliminate stupid mistakes."*

Think of it like a sports team watching game film. They do not just celebrate wins and forget losses. They review every play to find what worked, what did not, and how to improve. The best investors do the same thing.

## The Patience Principle

Investing in turnarounds requires patience that most people do not have. Kyler typically holds for 1-4 years. During that time, the stock might go sideways for months. It might drop significantly before it recovers. The approach, as Kyler puts it, *"isn't smooth or index-like."*

> **Key Idea:** *"Inactivity is normal. Patience is part of the edge."* Most investors feel like they need to be doing something -- buying, selling, adjusting. Kyler recognizes that often the best action is no action at all. If the thesis is intact, the catalysts are progressing, and the position is sized correctly, the right move is to wait.

This does not mean ignoring the portfolio. It means doing the monitoring work described above and then having the discipline to sit still when the conclusion is "nothing has changed."

The 2024 portfolio review makes this concrete. Kyler wrote about how his biggest lesson was stopping the habit of moving in and out of positions based on short-term news: *"In years prior I would often move in and out of positions based on momentum and short term news."*

He describes the temptation to chase new ideas as "the woman in the red dress" -- an analogy from Alex Hormozi about the next interesting opportunity that tempts you to abandon your current one. With over 12,000 stocks listed in the U.S. alone, there is always something that looks exciting. The discipline is staying with your researched positions instead of chasing the new shiny thing.

> *"After all, out of the richest investors of all time not a single one is a day trader."*

## Kyler's Journey: From Blowing Up to Discipline

Understanding where Kyler came from makes his current system more meaningful.

At 16, Kyler convinced his parents to open a brokerage account. He had no system, no rules, and no analysis. He chased trends, bought hype, and blew up his account more than once.

One story stands out: after spending 5-10 hours learning about options, he bought a call option expiring in two days and doubled his money. Feeling like a genius, he went bigger -- buying a Boeing call option expiring the next day. It went up 200% that afternoon. The next morning, Boeing opened down, and the option expired worthless.

> *"In reality, I had no strategy, no rules, and no analysis. And it showed pretty quickly."*

Those painful losses pushed him to learn the craft. Over years of study, he built the framework described in this series. By 2024, he was up 85.23%. By the end of 2025, his cumulative return was 305.34%.

The point is not the returns. The point is the transformation: from a 16-year-old gambling on next-day options to a disciplined investor who holds concentrated positions for years, reviews every mistake, and honestly steps away from strategies (like options) when his skills are not good enough.

---

## The Bottom Line

After the buy, investing becomes a psychological game. The market will test you daily with fear, greed, boredom, and the constant temptation of the next big thing.

Kyler's defense against all of this is built on three pillars:

1. **Information over emotion.** Fear cannot survive deep understanding of your holdings. Do the work, and the work protects you.

2. **Clear selling rules.** You sell when the math says so (forward returns too low) or when the facts change (thesis broken). Not when you feel scared.

3. **Continuous learning.** Every closed position becomes a lesson. The feedback loop is how the system improves year after year.

> *"Conviction doesn't come from having a high CAGR estimate in a spreadsheet. It comes from understanding how the real business is evolving over time. And that means staying close to the story."*
