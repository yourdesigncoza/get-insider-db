# Step 1: Finding Ideas

*Before Kyler dives into research or valuation, he needs names on a list. This step isn't about quality or conviction. It's about gathering potential threads to pull.*

---

## How Kyler's Approach Has Evolved

Kyler used to keep a massive **watchlist** (a list of stocks you're tracking -- see [09-GLOSSARY.md](09-GLOSSARY.md)) of any company he found interesting. The problem? The list became far too large to track or think about meaningfully.

Today, he takes a **top-down** approach. Instead of hunting for individual stocks one by one, he starts by looking at entire industries and sectors that are underperforming or have a clear **catalyst** (an upcoming event or trend that could change the market's perception of a company or industry).

> **Key Idea:** Think of it like shopping. The old approach was wandering through every aisle of a massive store, picking up anything that caught his eye. The new approach is walking into the store knowing he wants to find deals in the "home improvement" section because there's a renovation boom coming.

### The Two Conditions

Before Kyler even looks at an industry, it must pass two basic tests:

1. **It must be understandable to him.** He doesn't need to know every detail yet, but he should have a basic idea of how companies in that industry make money and what risks they face.

2. **It must not be in secular decline.** A **secular decline** (see [09-GLOSSARY.md](09-GLOSSARY.md)) is when an entire industry is shrinking over the long term -- not just having a bad year, but permanently losing ground. Think of how streaming replaced DVD rental stores.

> "Rowing against a river can be done, but it is far more difficult than moving with the current."

---

## The Natural Gas Example: Top-Down in Action

In early 2024, Kyler noticed something interesting about natural gas:

- Gas prices had fallen steeply
- The entire sector was deeply unloved by investors
- But two massive demand drivers were coming: **AI-driven energy demand** (data centers need enormous amounts of power) and **LNG exports** (liquefied natural gas being shipped overseas)

This is the top-down approach in action. He didn't start by finding one natural gas company and getting excited. He identified an entire sector where:
- Prices were depressed (potential value)
- Investors had given up (potential mispricing)
- Real catalysts were on the horizon (reasons for prices to recover)

From there, he went deeper to evaluate specific companies within natural gas.

## From Sector to Stocks: Building the Short List

When Kyler finds a promising sector, he digs into individual companies, building a smaller list of businesses that appear to be good or great quality and have the potential to be a "double-plus" (a stock that could more than double) over the next 2-3 years.

### The Quality Check

At this early stage, "quality" is based on three things:

- **Leverage** (see [09-GLOSSARY.md](09-GLOSSARY.md)): How much debt does the company carry relative to its size? Too much debt in a beaten-down industry is dangerous.
- **Returns on capital** (see ROIC/ROCE in [09-GLOSSARY.md](09-GLOSSARY.md)): Is the company good at turning money into more money? This tells you if the business itself works well.
- **Growth vs. share count changes**: Is the company actually growing, or is revenue only going up because they're buying other companies with newly issued shares? You want real per-share growth.

### The Double-Plus Potential Check

To estimate whether a stock could double or more, Kyler looks at:

- **Historical margins**: What profit margins did the company earn in normal times? If margins have fallen from 10% to 3%, there's room to recover.
- **Historical valuation multiples** (see [09-GLOSSARY.md](09-GLOSSARY.md)): What price-to-earnings or price-to-cash-flow ratios did the market assign in normal times? If the multiple has also compressed, there's a double benefit when things normalize -- both profits and the multiple can expand.

> **Key Idea:** When margins fall, multiples often compress too. That creates a "coiled spring" effect. Estimating what a "normalized" margin and multiple might look like gives Kyler a rough sense of whether a stock could double or more.

### Grouping by Theme

As Kyler finds promising names, he groups them by theme or industry. This lets him compare companies against each other later. He uses **Stock Unlock** to track what he's already reviewed and to screen for ideas within the industries he's focused on. He chose it for its long-term data coverage, low cost, and access to global markets.

## Other Idea Sources

Not every investment comes from the top-down approach. Kyler also pulls ideas from:

- **Substack** writers who go deep on sectors that don't get much attention
- **YouTube** channels focused on undervalued companies
- **Podcasts** with non-traditional investors
- **News articles** that point to trends he hadn't considered

When he hears about a company from these sources, it goes into a "first-look bucket." If it fits a theme he's already researching, it moves into that group for comparison. If it doesn't fit a theme, it stays in the bucket for later.

> **Key Idea:** "The edge doesn't start with analysis. It starts with doing the gathering consistently." Kyler is always gathering names -- some days that means a couple hours of searching, other times it's just a few quick notes after a podcast.

## The Broken Charts Pattern

One pattern Kyler respects: **broken charts**.

If a stock is down 60%, 80%, or more from its all-time highs, that doesn't automatically mean it's a buy. But it tells Kyler something important: *the market has moved on*. The story is probably broken. And broken stories are where he often finds mispricing.

**The Garage Sale Analogy**

At a garage sale, items are cheap because the owner wants them gone, not because they're worthless. A barely-used power tool for $5 might be a steal. A broken toaster for $5 is still junk. Broken charts are like the garage sale -- most items are junk, but mixed in you can find real value that other people overlooked because they lumped everything together as "stuff nobody wants."

Cooper Standard ($CPS) is a perfect example. Its stock fell from $145 to under $4 -- a 97% drop -- because of solvency concerns. The market thought it was dead. But the business was still generating revenue, cutting costs, and refinancing debt. Kyler saw a quality company at a garage-sale price.

## Industries Kyler Avoids

These industries fall outside Kyler's **circle of competence** (the range of businesses you genuinely understand well enough to evaluate -- see [09-GLOSSARY.md](09-GLOSSARY.md)) or he avoids due to their capital-destructive nature:

| Industry | Why He Avoids It |
|---|---|
| Pure AI | Too speculative, hard to value, outside his competence |
| Airlines | Capital-destructive, razor-thin margins, commodity pricing |
| Banks | Complex balance sheets, hard to evaluate risk |
| Biotech | Binary outcomes (drug works or doesn't), outside his competence |
| Car Manufacturers | Cyclical, capital-intensive, low returns on capital |
| Insurance | Complex (float, reserves), requires specialized knowledge |
| Marine Freight | Volatile, commodity-driven, hard to predict |
| Precious Metal Miners | Commodity prices out of anyone's control |
| Restaurants | High failure rates, intense competition, low margins |
| Tobacco | Secular decline in smoking |
| Textiles | Low barriers, race-to-the-bottom pricing |
| Trading Firms | Opaque, unpredictable |
| Most Software | Often overvalued, hard to assess durability |
| Video Games | Hit-driven, unpredictable revenue |

It's not that these are un-investable. Kyler just knows he's not the right person to evaluate them. Sticking to what you understand is more valuable than chasing something you don't.

## Willing to Hold Cash

One underrated part of Kyler's approach: he's willing to hold cash if he can't find attractive opportunities.

> "If I can't find attractive opportunities, I'm willing to hold cash, even though I prefer not to."

Most investors feel pressure to always be "doing something" with their money. They buy mediocre stocks just to feel invested. Kyler would rather sit on cash and wait for a great opportunity than force money into a bad one. This patience is a real competitive advantage.

## The Earlier Approach (For Context)

In earlier versions of his framework, Kyler also used these techniques (and may still use them occasionally):

- **Living watchlist with price targets**: If he liked a company but it was too expensive, he'd set a target price in Stock Unlock. When the stock dropped to that level, it automatically moved back onto his radar.
- **Screeners on Finviz and Stock Unlock**: He'd sort by P/FCF (price to free cash flow) or P/S (price to sales), filter for stocks down significantly from highs, or scroll through beaten-down sectors alphabetically.
- **Broken chart hunting**: Specifically looking for stocks down 60-80%+ as a starting point for investigation.

These still play a role, but the top-down sector approach is now the primary method.
