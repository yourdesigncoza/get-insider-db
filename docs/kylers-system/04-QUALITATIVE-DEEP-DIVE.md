# Beyond the Numbers: Moats, Management, and Catalysts

*Step 4 of Kyler's system. The numbers got the company this far. Now it's time to understand the things a spreadsheet can't tell you.*

---

## Why This Step Exists

Numbers tell you *what happened*. Qualitative analysis tells you *why it happened* and *whether it will keep happening*.

Think of it this way: if you were hiring someone for a job, you wouldn't just look at their test scores. You'd want to know: Are they reliable? Do they work well under pressure? Do they have skills that are hard to replace?

That's what Kyler does with companies at this stage. He reads reports, listens to earnings calls, and tries to understand the *character* of the business and the people running it.

> **Key Idea:** "This is where numbers stop being enough."

This is the most time-intensive step in Kyler's process. It's also where many of his strongest convictions -- and strongest rejections -- come from.

---

## The Five Questions Kyler Asks

### 1. Does the Company Have Durable Competitive Advantages?

A **competitive advantage** is something that lets a company earn more money than its rivals. A **durable** competitive advantage (also called an **economic moat**) is one that lasts for years or decades because it's extremely hard for competitors to copy (see [Glossary](09-GLOSSARY.md)).

> **Key Idea:** An economic moat and a competitive advantage are different. A competitive advantage lets a company *currently* earn a premium. An economic moat is a *sustainable* competitive advantage -- one that persists over time.

Imagine a castle surrounded by a deep, wide moat filled with water. Attackers can't easily get in. That's what a moat does for a business -- it keeps competitors from stealing its profits.

Kyler looks for several types:

#### Low-Cost Production

The company can make products cheaper than anyone else, so even if prices drop, it still makes money.

**Kyler's example -- Costco:** "Costco's philosophy: 'Keep costs down and pass the savings on to our members.' Costco has a gross margin of 12.6% while their average competitor has a gross margin of 25.8%." Despite charging customers *less*, Costco generates strong returns because of its enormous buying power and relentless efficiency. Its customers are fiercely loyal as a result.

**How to spot it:** Look for a company in a commodity-type business (where products are similar) that generates similar or better returns than competitors while charging customers less.

#### Regulatory Barriers

Government rules make it nearly impossible for new competitors to enter the market.

**Kyler's example -- Union Pacific (UNP):** Building a new railroad would require "a large amount of land crossing many counties and states often requiring many levels of approval." You'd need permission from dozens of businesses, regulatory boards, residents, environmental agencies, states, and counties. Good luck.

**How to spot it:** Look for industries where government licenses, permits, or approvals create barriers so high that no rational business would try to enter.

#### Switching Costs

It's so expensive or painful for customers to switch to a competitor that they don't bother.

**Kyler's example -- Microsoft (MSFT):** "Many of their customers have their business highly integrated with Teams and Office that would require lots of capital and time to unwind, along with the headache of the transition." Once a company has built its entire workflow around Microsoft products, switching to Google or another platform is a massive, costly undertaking.

**How to spot it:** Look for products that are deeply embedded in customers' daily operations. The more integrated the product, the higher the switching cost.

#### Network Effects

The product becomes more valuable as more people use it. This creates a self-reinforcing loop -- more users attract even more users.

**Kyler's example -- Meta (META):** "Meta is the perfect example of a network effect where more users mean more value for others looking for a platform. This will continue to build on itself as more and more people join Facebook, Instagram or WhatsApp."

**How to spot it:** Look for platforms where the value to each user increases as the total number of users grows. Social networks, marketplaces, and payment systems often have this quality.

#### Capital Requirements

It costs so much money to enter the industry that almost nobody tries.

**Kyler's example -- Boeing (BA):** "Boeing's planes can cost on the high end $450 million per plane, and yet the company's peak FCF margin sits at 13.9%, meaning it probably cost them $387 million to build that one plane. This means a new entrant would need at least a few tens of billions of dollars to get things running."

Boeing and Airbus together control virtually the entire large commercial aircraft market. No serious competitor has emerged in decades because the upfront investment is astronomical and it takes years to win regulatory approval.

**How to spot it:** Look for industries where the entry ticket is billions of dollars, timelines stretch over years, and scale is required to be profitable.

#### Brand Strength

Customers trust the brand enough to pay more for it or to keep buying it habitually.

**Kyler's example -- Hormel:** Hormel owns "more than 40 of the most recognizable brands in their category" -- SPAM, Skippy, Planters, Jennie-O, Applegate, Hormel Black Label. In packaged food, "brands do not eliminate competition, but they meaningfully influence consumer behavior, pricing power, and long-term performance across cycles." Customers buy Skippy peanut butter by habit, not by comparing prices every week.

**How to spot it:** Does the company own brands that consumers buy repeatedly without much thought? Can it raise prices without losing significant volume?

> **Key Idea:** "Identifying moats will take a lot of work. Spend lots of time reading annual reports of several competitors in the industry, getting to understand where each company lies. Study current brands with obvious moats. Study their stories from when they were just starting and then look for those same signs in other companies."

### 2. Does Leadership Have a Positive Operating History?

Kyler asks two specific questions about management:

1. **Does the CEO have a track record of generating shareholder returns** -- in both their current role and past roles?
2. **Is the CEO the right person for this specific situation?**

> **Key Idea:** "Finding a quality business led by a great CEO is different. That combination is often a recipe for success."

Think of it like a sports team. A good team with an average coach can still win games. But a good team with a *great* coach becomes a dynasty. Kyler is looking for that coach.

**Kyler's example -- Kelly Ortberg at Boeing:** Ortberg is perhaps Kyler's best example of management quality driving a thesis. Before Boeing, Ortberg led Rockwell Collins from 2013 to 2018:

- Revenue nearly doubled from $4.6 billion to ~$8-9 billion
- He maintained ~20% operating margins while integrating major acquisitions
- EPS rose every year he was in charge
- The stock more than doubled, outperforming the S&P 500
- He had an 89% employee approval rating and was named one of Glassdoor's Top 100 CEOs

At Boeing, Ortberg moved quickly: resolved a machinist strike, raised $24.3 billion to stabilize the balance sheet, reacquired Spirit AeroSystems for quality control, pushed a cultural reset encouraging employees to flag safety concerns, and won key FAA approvals to increase production.

> "'I'm not pressuring the team to go fast. I'm pressuring the team to do it right.' -- Kelly Ortberg"

**Kyler's example -- Naomi James at Ryman Healthcare:** Before joining Ryman, James was CEO of Channel Infrastructure in New Zealand. She took over a broken refining business during COVID, exited refining entirely, and repositioned it as a simpler logistics business. Channel's stock rose over 600% from the lows during her tenure. At Ryman, she immediately focused on deleveraging, cost discipline, and cash generation -- the same playbook.

**What Kyler watches for:**
- Has the CEO been in similar situations before and succeeded?
- Is the CEO an operator (good at fixing things) or a visionary (good at building new things)? Turnarounds need operators.
- Does the CEO's background match the company's biggest challenge? (Boeing needed an engineer, not a finance person.)

### 3. What Issues Is the Company Facing, and How Are They Addressing Them?

> **Key Idea:** "Turnarounds are labeled that way for a reason. They've gone through something that caused shareholders to exit."

Kyler makes a list of everything that went wrong:

- Declining revenue?
- Margin compression?
- Loss of market share?
- Shareholder dilution?
- Solvency concerns?
- Regulatory changes?
- Some combination of all of these?

Then he asks: **What is management doing, specifically, to fix each problem?**

Vague answers like "we're working on it" don't count. Kyler wants to see concrete actions with measurable results.

**Real example -- Boeing's problems and fixes:**

| Problem | Specific Fix |
|---|---|
| Quality failures (crashes, door plug incident) | Hired engineering CEO (Ortberg), reacquired Spirit AeroSystems for quality control, reduced "traveled work" (rework) by 75% on the 737 line |
| Regulatory production caps | Brought all six FAA-monitored quality indicators back to green, received approval to increase production to 42 planes/month |
| Massive debt ($53.4B) | Raised $24.3B in equity, sold Jeppesen business for $10B, maintained $10B in undrawn credit |
| Cultural problems | Implemented direct feedback loops with frontline employees, pushed accountability |

**Real example -- Hormel's problems and fixes:**

| Problem | Specific Fix |
|---|---|
| Input cost inflation outpacing pricing | Implemented price increases (lagged, but flowing through) |
| Depressed margins | Transform & Modernize (T&M) initiative delivered ~$200M in cumulative savings |
| Operational disruptions (Planters facility) | Facility restored; Planters distribution up 13% YoY |
| Organizational complexity | Cut ~9% of corporate and sales roles, rationalized SKU portfolio |

### 4. What Is Management's Compensation Tied To?

This is where Kyler channels Charlie Munger:

> "*Show me the incentives, and I will show you the outcome.*" -- Charlie Munger

**Compensation incentives** are the financial rewards management receives for hitting certain targets. If the CEO gets a bonus for growing revenue, they'll focus on growing revenue. If they get a bonus for cutting costs, they'll cut costs. The question is: **do these incentives align with what shareholders want?**

Kyler looks at what metrics drive executive pay:

- **EPS (Earnings Per Share)?** Good, but can be manipulated through accounting tricks or share buybacks.
- **FCF (Free Cash Flow)?** Better -- harder to fake and more directly tied to real business performance.
- **Revenue targets?** Fine for growth, but meaningless if the company is bleeding money.
- **EBITDA margins?** Decent, but has room for manipulation.

**Red flags in management behavior:**

- **Broken promises:** Management said they'd hit a target and didn't, repeatedly
- **Vague language:** Lots of buzzwords without concrete plans or numbers
- **Overly promotional communication:** More focused on hype than substance

> **Key Idea:** "If management has a history of broken promises, vague language, or overly promotional communication, I have to decide whether the company is a permanent pass or requires a lower price."

In other words, bad management doesn't always mean Kyler walks away -- but it means he needs a bigger discount to take the risk.

**Real example -- Tactile Systems:** Kyler noted that both short-term cash bonuses and long-term equity bonuses are tied to revenue targets and Adjusted EBITDA margin improvements. His take: "I love the revenue target incentivizing growth paired with margin bonuses but would prefer a metric that has less opportunity to be manipulated, like FCF. Overall I think the bonus structure is slightly better than most because all bonuses are tied to measurable targets, but it is not perfect."

### 5. What Catalysts Could Change the Business or Market Perception?

This is one of Kyler's most important requirements. He won't invest without it.

> **Key Idea:** "For any investment I make, there need to be catalysts coming down the pipeline that challenge the existing narrative. Otherwise, **cheap companies can remain cheap for a very long time.**"

A **catalyst** is an event or trend that changes either the actual business performance or the market's *perception* of the business (see [Glossary](09-GLOSSARY.md)). It's the reason the stock price should eventually go up.

Think of a catalyst like a spark. A pile of firewood has potential energy, but without a spark, it just sits there. A cheap stock has potential upside, but without a catalyst, it can stay cheap for years.

**Types of catalysts Kyler looks for:**

| Catalyst Type | What It Means | Real Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Margin expansion** | Costs going down or prices going up | Hormel's T&M initiative saving $200M+ |
| **Regulatory clearance** | Government approval unlocking growth | Boeing getting FAA approval to increase production |
| **New leadership** | A strong CEO taking over a struggling company | Kelly Ortberg at Boeing, Naomi James at Ryman |
| **Faster growth** | Revenue accelerating beyond expectations | Alibaba Cloud growing 34% YoY with AI revenue tripling |
| **Falling interest rates** | Lower rates improving borrowing costs and asset values | NZ rate cuts from 5.5% to 2.25% helping Ryman |
| **Demographic or FX tailwinds** | Population trends or currency shifts helping the business | NZ's 75+ population growing 40% by 2033 (Ryman); rising protein demand (Hormel) |
| **Demand drivers** | New sources of demand emerging | GLP-1 drugs increasing protein consumption (Hormel); AI driving cloud demand (Alibaba) |
| **Divestitures** | Selling off bad parts to focus on good parts | Boeing selling Jeppesen for $10B to focus on aircraft |
| **Combination** | Multiple catalysts converging | Most common -- Kyler says this is "more often" the case |

> **Key Idea:** "If I struggle to find any catalysts, I will always pass."

**Real example -- Alibaba's catalysts (a combination):**

Alibaba shows how multiple catalysts can stack:
1. **Policy shift:** Chinese government moved from punishing tech companies to partnering with them
2. **Demographic tailwinds:** Wave of Chinese citizens aging into peak spending years (35-44 bracket)
3. **Cloud acceleration:** AI cloud revenue growing triple digits for 9 consecutive quarters
4. **Instant commerce:** Market share growing from near-zero to 30-40% in under a year
5. **Capital return:** $19.1 billion remaining in share buyback program

No single catalyst made the thesis. The convergence of all five created the setup Kyler was looking for.

**Real example -- Boeing's catalysts:**
1. **New CEO** executing quality fixes
2. **FAA approvals** unlocking production ramps (from 38 to 42 planes/month, targeting 52)
3. **777X certification** expected in 2027, opening a new revenue stream
4. **Geopolitical tailwinds** driving foreign orders
5. **Spirit AeroSystems reacquisition** improving quality control

## How This Connects to the Competitor Comparison

Kyler doesn't just do this deep dive for his top pick. He follows the same process, "though at a lighter level," with his next-best ideas from the comparison step ([see 03-DEEP-ANALYSIS.md](03-DEEP-ANALYSIS.md)).

After doing the qualitative work on multiple companies:

> "I decide which opportunity is truly the best and move forward accordingly."

This additional layer often reshuffles the rankings. A company that looked best on paper might have terrible management. A second-place company might have a CEO with a proven turnaround record. The qualitative dive frequently changes the final answer.

---

## Quick Summary

| Question | What Kyler Wants to See |
|---|---|
| **Competitive advantages / moats?** | Something difficult to replicate: low cost, regulation, switching costs, network effects, capital requirements, brand strength |
| **Leadership track record?** | Positive operating history with measurable shareholder returns in current and past roles |
| **What went wrong and what's the fix?** | Concrete, specific actions with early evidence of results -- not vague promises |
| **What drives management's pay?** | Incentives aligned with shareholders; measurable targets; transparency and honesty |
| **What catalysts exist?** | One or more forces that will change the business or market perception; without catalysts, Kyler always passes |

---

## The Bottom Line

The qualitative deep dive is where Kyler separates companies that *look* good from companies that *are* good. Numbers can be misleading -- a cheap stock with no moat, bad management, and no catalysts will stay cheap (or get cheaper).

But a cheap stock with a durable competitive advantage, a proven CEO, concrete fixes for its problems, aligned incentives, and multiple catalysts converging? That's a setup Kyler will bet on.
